<p>The post <a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/behind-aaros-private-uap-workshop-new-records-detail-participants-foreign-outreach-and-future-plans/">Behind AARO’s Private UAP Workshop: New Records Detail Participants, Foreign Outreach and Future Plans</a> first appeared on <a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive">The Black Vault</a>.</p>]]></description>
										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/AARO-Logo-2025-01.png"><img fetchpriority="high" decoding="async" class="alignright size-medium wp-image-21516" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/AARO-Logo-2025-01-300x267.png" alt="" width="300" height="267" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/AARO-Logo-2025-01-300x267.png 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/AARO-Logo-2025-01-1024x912.png 1024w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/AARO-Logo-2025-01-1536x1369.png 1536w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/AARO-Logo-2025-01-2048x1825.png 2048w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/AARO-Logo-2025-01-150x134.png 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/AARO-Logo-2025-01-450x401.png 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/AARO-Logo-2025-01-1200x1069.png 1200w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/AARO-Logo-2025-01-768x684.png 768w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/AARO-Logo-2025-01-600x535.png 600w" sizes="(max-width: 300px) 100vw, 300px" /></a>A newly released collection of records from the Pentagon’s All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office provides a substantially more detailed look at the government-sponsored UAP research workshop held outside Washington, D.C., in August 2025, including a working agenda identifying speakers and panelists, previously unreleased correspondence concerning foreign participation, and evidence that AARO was already discussing a follow-on workshop months before the first event took place.</p>
<p>The records also document a separate Pentagon engagement with representatives of Uruguay’s official UAP investigative organization, including plans for reciprocal mission briefings, discussions about future collaboration, lunch with senior AARO personnel and a private Pentagon tour.</p>
<p>The documents were released to The Black Vault under Freedom of Information Act case 26-F-1773. The request was filed March 17, 2026, one day after <a href="https://defensescoop.com/2026/03/16/dod-ufo-workshop-uap-research-aaro/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DefenseScoop</a> senior reporter <a href="https://x.com/BrandiVincent_" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Brandi Vincent</a> broke the story of the invitation-only workshop and obtained additional information about it from the Pentagon. Vincent’s original reporting established that approximately 40 government, academic and independent researchers had participated in the AARO-sponsored gathering and detailed its emphasis on integrating narrative UAP data and applying artificial intelligence to large datasets.</p>
<p>The new FOIA release does not fundamentally alter the publicly stated purpose or findings of the workshop. Instead, its significance is in filling in portions of the administrative record that AARO’s public white paper left anonymous or generalized: who delivered some of the presentations, who sat on the panels, where the workshop actually occurred, and how AARO interacted with at least one foreign government UAP organization before the event.</p>
<p>It is not a complete record. AARO identified 89 responsive pages but withheld 19 pages in their entirety under <a href="https://www.justice.gov/archives/oip/foia-guide-2004-edition-exemption-5" target="_blank" rel="noopener">FOIA Exemption (b)(5)</a>, which protects certain deliberative government communications. Portions of the other 70 pages were withheld under Exemptions (b)(5) and <a href="https://www.justice.gov/archives/oip/foia-guide-2004-edition-exemption-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener">(b)(6)</a>, with the latter protecting personal privacy.</p>
<p><strong>From a Public White Paper to the Records Behind It</strong></p>
<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-25_05-46-27.jpg"><img decoding="async" class="alignleft size-medium wp-image-21517" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-25_05-46-27-300x235.jpg" alt="" width="300" height="235" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-25_05-46-27-300x235.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-25_05-46-27-150x118.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-25_05-46-27-450x353.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-25_05-46-27-768x603.jpg 768w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-25_05-46-27-600x471.jpg 600w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-25_05-46-27.jpg 827w" sizes="(max-width: 300px) 100vw, 300px" /></a>AARO publicly released its <a href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/Information%20Papers/2025_UAP_Workshop_Paper.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">2025 UAP Workshop: Narrative Data, Infrastructures, and Analysis</a> paper in February 2026. The official document described an August 5-6, 2025 workshop sponsored by AARO and hosted by Associated Universities, Inc., or AUI, bringing together 40 participants from government, academia and independent research organizations.</p>
<p>The workshop concentrated primarily on an unusually difficult UAP research problem: narrative reports.</p>
<p>Unlike standardized sensor data, witness accounts can originate in military reports, civilian reporting systems, archives, social media and other sources using different terminology, formats and levels of documentation. The workshop considered methods for standardizing those records, connecting databases and using artificial intelligence and machine learning to process large quantities of material.</p>
<p>Some participants had already publicly acknowledged attending. The National UFO Reporting Center <a href="https://nuforc.org/nuforc-aaro-workshop/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">announced</a> its participation on August 11, 2025, just days after the meeting, and later identified Chief Technical Officer Christian Stepien as a participant.</p>
<p>The Society for UAP Studies also subsequently <a href="https://www.societyforuapstudies.org/post/society-for-uap-studies-newsletter" target="_blank" rel="noopener">disclosed</a> its involvement and identified several participants. Its account said Michael Cifone delivered a presentation titled It’s All About the Evidence and identified Robert Powell of the Scientific Coalition for UAP Studies among the speakers.</p>
<p>Those disclosures are important because the workshop was private, but its existence and some of its participants were not secret after it occurred.</p>
<p><strong>The Working Agenda Names Speakers and Panelists</strong></p>
<p>One of the clearest additions is an August 5, 2025, working agenda identifying people whose names and affiliations were omitted from the generic agenda later published in AARO’s white paper.</p>
<p>The newly released agenda identifies the workshop location as Associated Universities Inc., 2650 Park Tower Drive, Suite 700, Vienna, Virginia, across from the Dunn Loring Metro station.</p>
<p>Opening remarks were scheduled from AUI representatives <a href="https://aui.edu/auis-tim-spuck-co-editor-einstein-fellows/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Tim Spuck</a> and <a href="https://aui.edu/trustees/adam-cohen/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Adam Cohen</a>, AARO Director Jon Kosloski, and <a href="https://directory.cci.fsu.edu/gretchen-stahlman/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Gretchen Stahlman</a> of Florida State University’s School of Information.</p>
<p>The keynote, titled “Setting the stage: Importance of good UAP data,” was assigned to Robert Powell of the <a href="https://www.explorescu.org/about-us" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Scientific Coalition for UAP Studies</a> and Kosloski.</p>
<p>The agenda then names <a href="https://www.explorescu.org/lauradomine" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Laura Domine</a>, at the time identified as a postdoctoral fellow at the Center for Astrophysics, Harvard University, for the first session.</p>
<p>Domine’s participation fits directly with the workshop’s technical focus. Her published research includes work on the Galileo Project’s multi-sensor systems for collecting scientific observations of aerial phenomena, including an infrared camera array designed to continuously monitor and classify objects in the sky.</p>
<p>The first panel, “Opportunities and challenges with AI,” was to be moderated by Spuck. The working agenda identifies four panelists:</p>
<ul>
<li><a href="https://substack.com/@presentofcoding" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Abigail Haddad</a>, identified as a machine-learning engineer;</li>
<li><a href="https://ischool.utexas.edu/profiles/elliott-hauser" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Elliott Hauser</a>, assistant professor at the University of Texas at Austin School of Information;</li>
<li><a href="https://maximus.com/employees/duncan-mccaskill" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Duncan McCaskill</a>, identified as formerly with the U.S. Navy Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Office;</li>
<li><a href="https://www.albany.edu/physics/faculty/matthew-szydagis" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Matthew Szydagis</a>, associate professor of physics at the University at Albany.</li>
</ul>
<p>The second day included a presentation by <a href="https://www.vumc.org/painresearch/person/stephen-bruehl-phd" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Stephen Breuhl</a> of Vanderbilt University Medical Center and another panel titled “Harmonizing qualitative and quantitative perspectives on narrative data.”</p>
<p>That panel was scheduled to include Cifone; <a href="https://impossiblearchives.rice.edu/amanda-focke" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Amanda Focke</a>, head of Special Collections at Rice University’s Fondren Library; <a href="https://cbengibson.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Ben Gibson</a>, identified as a social/behavioral scientist at RAND Corporation; and NUFORC’s <a href="https://nuforc.org/about-us/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Christian Stepien</a>.</p>
<p>The released agenda provides a more specific picture than AARO’s published workshop paper of the mixture of technical researchers, archivists, academics and established civilian UAP organizations invited into the discussion.</p>
<p><strong>AARO Was Also Engaging Uruguay’s Official UAP Organization</strong></p>
<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-25_05-47-33.jpg"><img decoding="async" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-21518" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-25_05-47-33.jpg" alt="" width="697" height="447" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-25_05-47-33.jpg 697w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-25_05-47-33-300x192.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-25_05-47-33-150x96.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-25_05-47-33-450x289.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-25_05-47-33-600x385.jpg 600w" sizes="(max-width: 697px) 100vw, 697px" /></a></p>
<p>Some of the more substantive new material concerns CRIDOVNI, Uruguay&#8217;s official commission for receiving and investigating reports of unidentified aerial phenomena within the Uruguayan Air Force.</p>
<p>Names of the foreign participants are redacted from the FOIA release under Exemption (b)(6), so the documents themselves do not provide a basis for identifying those individuals by name.</p>
<p>In a July 2025 email, an AARO partner-engagement officer told the visitors that AARO would provide hotel accommodations for several nights and proposed a separate August 4 meeting at the Pentagon, the day before the workshop began.</p>
<p>The stated purpose went beyond workshop logistics.</p>
<p>“On 4 August (Monday), would you two be available for a meeting at the Pentagon to provide briefings on our respective work at CRIDOVNI and AARO?”</p>
<p>The AARO official proposed discussing “the types of UAP data our offices analyze, types of analysis we undertake, and ways our offices can collaborate moving forward.”</p>
<p>The Uruguayan correspondent responded affirmatively:</p>
<p>“On August 4, of course, we will be available for a meeting at the Pentagon, it is of special interest to us to be able to establish a further working relationship with the AARO, and to exchange information and experiences.”</p>
<p>The correspondent also accepted an invitation to present CRIDOVNI’s work during the workshop.</p>
<p><strong>Mission Briefings and “Future Collaboration” at the Pentagon</strong></p>
<p>Later correspondence shows the Pentagon visit was approved through the Foreign Visit Request and Pentagon Force Protection Agency visitor systems.</p>
<p>A July 29 email laid out the planned meeting:</p>
<p>“1000-1030 CRIDOVNI Mission Briefing<br />
1030-1100 AARO Mission Briefing<br />
1100-1200 Discussion of Briefings and Future Collaboration”</p>
<p>AARO then planned to host the visitors for lunch and a private Pentagon tour.</p>
<p>AARO also provided a detailed list of what it wanted to learn from CRIDOVNI, including its investigative process, analytical methods and tools, handling of cases containing little or no technical data, notable cases in its database, its organizational relationship with the Uruguayan Air Force, and its scientific and governmental partnerships.</p>
<p>Another email states that a Pentagon lunch reservation had been arranged involving Kosloski and AARO Chief Technology Officer Josh Reiter.</p>
<p>The records do not establish what ultimately resulted from the discussions, whether any formal information-sharing arrangement was reached, or whether the contemplated collaboration continued afterward. No such conclusion can be drawn from the released pages.</p>
<p>They do establish that AARO was actively discussing future cooperation with CRIDOVNI and exchanging information about how the two organizations investigated UAP.</p>
<p><strong>AARO Wanted More South American Participation</strong></p>
<p>The correspondence also provides a glimpse into AARO’s effort to expand participation beyond the United States.</p>
<p>In one email, the AARO official wrote:</p>
<p>“We currently do not have any other registered guests from South America.”</p>
<p>The official said an invitation had been sent through a Chilean contact in Washington in an effort to bring in participants from SEFAA, but that no response had been received.</p>
<p>AARO then asked the Uruguayan representatives for contacts with other South American UAP research organizations that could potentially be invited to a later event.</p>
<p>That request led directly to another notable disclosure.</p>
<p><strong>A Follow-On Workshop Was Already Being Planned</strong></p>
<p>The released email states:</p>
<p>“AARO plans to hold a follow-on data workshop in early 2026, so if you have contact information for other UAP research organizations in South America that you could share with us, we would very much appreciate it and like to extend invitations to them as well.”</p>
<p>The same message described most participants in the 2025 gathering as coming from “U.S.-based universities or UAP research organizations, along with several people from U.S. federal agencies.”</p>
<p>The reference is notable when compared with what the Pentagon publicly said months later.</p>
<p>In Vincent’s March 16 DefenseScoop report, Pentagon spokesperson Sue Gough said AARO hoped to convene future workshops and collaborative opportunities “as needed,” but DefenseScoop reported that Gough said AARO had no additional workshops or plans to announce at that time.</p>
<p>The two statements are not necessarily contradictory. The July 2025 email describes an internal plan for a follow-on workshop in early 2026; Gough’s March 2026 statement concerned what AARO had to announce publicly at that point. The released records do not establish whether the contemplated early-2026 event occurred, was postponed, changed form or was canceled.</p>
<p>They do establish that, before the first workshop had even taken place, AARO personnel were already telling prospective partners that another data workshop was planned.</p>
<p><strong>Hospitality Went Beyond the Formal Workshop</strong></p>
<p>The correspondence also shows the degree of personal and logistical attention provided to the Uruguayan visitors.</p>
<p>AARO arranged airport pickup, hotel accommodations and the Pentagon visit. The emails additionally discuss a Sunday Catholic Mass with Kosloski and the visitor and his wife. One message states:</p>
<p>“Perfect, Dr. Kosloski plans to pick you and your wife up from the hotel on August 3 and drive you to mass.”</p>
<p>The same email explains that Kosloski would locate a church near the visitors’ Virginia hotel.</p>
<p>These details do not bear directly on AARO’s UAP analysis, but they document the level of engagement surrounding the foreign delegation and provide context for a relationship that extended beyond simply registering two people for a conference.</p>
<p><strong>Nineteen Pages Withheld Completely</strong></p>
<p>The release also demonstrates the limits of what AARO was willing to disclose through FOIA.</p>
<p>The Black Vault’s request was intentionally broad. It sought attendee and invitation records, agendas, presentations, breakout materials, notes, transcripts, recordings, records concerning destruction of recordings, after-action reports, drafts of the public white paper, correspondence, contracts, funding records, IRB documentation and records concerning future workshops.</p>
<p>AARO reported locating 89 responsive pages.</p>
<p>Nineteen were withheld completely under Exemption (b)(5), with the agency describing them as inter- or intra-agency records that were deliberative and contained “subjective evaluations, opinions and recommendations.” Portions of the remaining 70 pages were withheld under the same exemption and under the personal-privacy provisions of Exemption (b)(6).</p>
<p>Several pages in the released package consist solely of a page number and a (b)(5) withholding notation, providing no indication of the underlying content.</p>
<p>Consequently, the release cannot be treated as a complete reconstruction of the workshop or AARO’s internal deliberations about it.</p>
<p><strong>The Public Paper Went Through Pentagon Review</strong></p>
<p>The records also include the Defense Department’s DD Form 1910 clearance paperwork for the eventual workshop paper.</p>
<p>The form identifies Kosloski as the author and describes the document as an 18-page white paper titled 2025 UAP Workshop: Narrative Data, Infrastructures, and Analysis. It states:</p>
<p>“This 18-page white paper captures findings and outcomes from an unclassified, AUI-hosted, AARO-sponsored interdisciplinary working group focused on narrative and technical UAP data integration.”</p>
<p>The form states that AUI intended to publish the paper on its website while AARO planned to promote it through social media and link to it from AARO.mil.</p>
<p><strong>A More Detailed Picture of AARO’s Outreach</strong></p>
<p>Taken together, the newly released material provides less of a revelation about what the workshop concluded than about how AARO assembled it and what the office was trying to build around it.</p>
<p>The public white paper already disclosed the methodological conclusions: standardize UAP data, preserve historical records, connect disparate databases, use AI cautiously, retain human oversight and develop a continuing interdisciplinary research community.</p>
<p>The FOIA records add names, affiliations and institutional relationships behind that summary. They show AARO putting its director on a keynote with a prominent civilian UAP researcher; bringing machine-learning specialists, physicists, archivists, social scientists and civilian UAP database operators into the same program; engaging Uruguay’s official UAP investigative body separately at the Pentagon; seeking contacts with additional South American organizations; and discussing another data workshop before the first one convened.</p>
<p>At the same time, the records leave significant gaps. AARO withheld 19 pages completely, redacted portions of others, and the released material does not establish the identities of all 40 participants, the full substance of the workshop discussions, the ultimate outcome of the proposed CRIDOVNI collaboration or what happened to the follow-on workshop contemplated for early 2026.</p>
<p>Additional FOIA requests are set to be filed regarding some of the above revelations, and those findings will be posted here at The Black Vault, when available.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p>The Department of the Navy has released more than 1,300 pages of emails and related records involving former Deputy Director of Naval Intelligence Scott W. Bray, opening a broad window into government communications surrounding unidentified aerial phenomena during a pivotal period in the Pentagon’s modern UAP effort.</p>
<p>The records were obtained by The Black Vault through a Freedom of Information Act request filed on May 24, 2022. The request sought emails sent to, from, copied to or blind-copied to Bray containing terms including “Unidentified Aerial Phenomena,” “UAP,” “UAPTF,” “UFO,” “AATIP,” “AAWSAP” and several related phrases.</p>
<p>More than four years later, the Navy responded on September 23, 2026. According to its response, the search located 1,321 pages of responsive records. The Navy withheld 302 pages in their entirety, while releasing the remaining 1,019 pages with redactions under several FOIA exemptions.</p>
<p><strong>Who Was Scott Bray?</strong></p>
<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/CKMZOMJW5VLVRFNLSVPGNAE7B4-scaled.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="alignright size-medium wp-image-21510" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/CKMZOMJW5VLVRFNLSVPGNAE7B4-300x225.jpg" alt="" width="300" height="225" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/CKMZOMJW5VLVRFNLSVPGNAE7B4-300x225.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/CKMZOMJW5VLVRFNLSVPGNAE7B4-1024x768.jpg 1024w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/CKMZOMJW5VLVRFNLSVPGNAE7B4-1536x1152.jpg 1536w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/CKMZOMJW5VLVRFNLSVPGNAE7B4-2048x1536.jpg 2048w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/CKMZOMJW5VLVRFNLSVPGNAE7B4-150x113.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/CKMZOMJW5VLVRFNLSVPGNAE7B4-450x338.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/CKMZOMJW5VLVRFNLSVPGNAE7B4-1200x900.jpg 1200w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/CKMZOMJW5VLVRFNLSVPGNAE7B4-768x576.jpg 768w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/CKMZOMJW5VLVRFNLSVPGNAE7B4-600x450.jpg 600w" sizes="(max-width: 300px) 100vw, 300px" /></a>Bray served as Deputy Director of Naval Intelligence and Director of the Naval Intelligence Activity during much of the period covered by the records. His position placed him within a network of Navy, Defense Department and Intelligence Community officials dealing with UAP reporting, briefings, congressional interest and the development of what ultimately became the Navy-led Unidentified Aerial Phenomena Task Force.</p>
<p>One email in the release described the relationship directly, stating that John “Jay” Stratton “leads the UAP Task Force effort for Navy&#8230; working directly for Scott Bray, our Deputy Director of Naval Intelligence.”</p>
<p>Bray later became one of the government&#8217;s most visible officials on the UAP issue. On May 17, 2022, he appeared with Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence and Security Ronald Moultrie before a House Intelligence subcommittee during a public congressional hearing devoted to UAP. The hearing was followed by a classified session.</p>
<p>The newly released records provide a much larger documentary backdrop to that public role.</p>
<p><strong>A Broad Look Behind the Government&#8217;s UAP Effort</strong></p>
<p>The correspondence stretches across years and includes exchanges involving Stratton, Defense Intelligence official Neill Tipton, senior Navy officials, admirals, Pentagon public-affairs personnel and other Defense and Intelligence Community officials.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_09-23-11.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-21512" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_09-23-11.jpg" alt="" width="801" height="417" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_09-23-11.jpg 801w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_09-23-11-300x156.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_09-23-11-150x78.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_09-23-11-450x234.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_09-23-11-768x400.jpg 768w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_09-23-11-600x312.jpg 600w" sizes="(max-width: 801px) 100vw, 801px" /></a></p>
<p>For example, a March 2020 exchange shows Tipton, who was then identified in his signature block as Director of Defense Intelligence, Collection and Special Programs in OUSD(I&amp;S), telling Bray that he had a document “relevant to our discussion the other day” that he preferred to deliver in hard copy. Other correspondence concerning a planned Senate Select Committee on Intelligence briefing includes Bray, Stratton, Tipton and Rear Adm. Kelly Aeschbach.</p>
<p>Elsewhere, the records show Stratton discussing proposed public language concerning the UAP effort and whether it should be reviewed by Bray before being sent forward. Other email traffic includes Vice Adm. Matthew Kohler, Rear Adm. Charles Brown and Rear Adm. Paula Dunn in communications surrounding the April 2020 public release of the Navy UAP videos.</p>
<p>Those are only examples of the names and subjects contained in the collection. The release includes communications surrounding congressional briefings, public-affairs strategy, the UAP Task Force, the 2021 ODNI UAP assessment, media inquiries, FOIA releases, classification questions, senior-level briefings and the government&#8217;s evolving organizational response to UAP.</p>
<p>There are also extensive attachments, forwarded news coverage and discussions of claims being made publicly about UAP. Their inclusion in Bray&#8217;s email records does not establish that the Navy or Bray endorsed those claims; in many instances, the records simply document what officials were receiving, discussing or preparing to address.</p>
<p><strong>A Large Record With Significant Gaps</strong></p>
<p>The release should not be viewed as a complete history of Bray&#8217;s involvement with UAP or of the UAP Task Force itself. The FOIA request was constructed around specific search terms, and the Navy withheld 302 responsive pages completely. Redactions also appear throughout the pages that were released.</p>
<p>What remains is nevertheless an unusually large collection of contemporaneous correspondence from inside the Navy and Defense Department during the years in which the modern UAP issue was developing into a formal, highly visible government effort.</p>
<p>Given the size of the release and the number of separate subjects contained within it, individual email chains and issues may warrant separate examination. The complete released collection is being made available through The Black Vault for independent review.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-17-23.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="alignright wp-image-21500 size-medium" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-17-23-300x300.jpg" alt="" width="300" height="300" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-17-23-300x300.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-17-23-150x150.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-17-23-450x451.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-17-23-600x602.jpg 600w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-17-23-336x336.jpg 336w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-17-23.jpg 647w" sizes="(max-width: 300px) 100vw, 300px" /></a>Newly released Central Intelligence Agency records provide the most complete look yet at two separate attempts by Jeffrey Epstein, which were more than a decade apart, to determine what information the CIA maintained about him.</p>
<p>The records were obtained by The Black Vault after case numbers for Epstein’s Privacy Act requests surfaced within the broader government release of Epstein-related files. Rather than requesting CIA records about Epstein specifically, The Black Vault used those case identifiers, listed as P-1999-02450 and P-2011-00673, to seek the records generated during the processing of the two cases. This also stemmed from a public <a href="https://x.com/MikeBenzCyber/status/2018599847176319368" target="_blank" rel="noopener">call to action</a> request from political commentator and speechwriter Mike Benz.</p>
<p>The CIA responded on September 18, 2026, under FOIA case F-2026-00889, saying it had located 17 responsive documents. One was released in full and 16 were released with portions withheld under FOIA Exemptions (b)(3) and/or (b)(6).</p>
<p>The resulting near 50-page release contains Epstein&#8217;s original 1999 request, internal CIA search records, correspondence between Epstein and the agency, and the records surrounding a substantially more specific request submitted on his behalf in 2011.</p>
<p>The documents establish that the CIA reported finding no records filed under Epstein&#8217;s name in response to his first request. In 2011, the agency similarly reported finding no records reflecting an open or otherwise acknowledged CIA affiliation during the period it searched. For records that could reveal a classified connection, however, the CIA gave a different response: it refused to confirm or deny whether such records existed.</p>
<p>That distinction is central to what the newly released records show, and what they do not.</p>
<p><strong>Epstein Asks the CIA for &#8220;All Records About Me&#8221;</strong></p>
<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-22-28.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-21501" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-22-28.jpg" alt="" width="939" height="454" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-22-28.jpg 939w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-22-28-300x145.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-22-28-150x73.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-22-28-450x218.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-22-28-768x371.jpg 768w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-22-28-600x290.jpg 600w" sizes="(max-width: 939px) 100vw, 939px" /></a></p>
<p>Epstein&#8217;s first request was dated October 28, 1999.</p>
<p>Writing directly to the CIA&#8217;s Information and Privacy Coordinator, Epstein invoked both the Freedom of Information Act and Privacy Act and made a broad request:</p>
<blockquote><p>&#8220;please furnish me with copies of all records about me indexed to my name.&#8221;</p></blockquote>
<p>Epstein supplied identifying information including his full name, date and place of birth, citizenship, current address and several other addresses. The released document also contained a Social Security number, which is not reproduced here in this written article, though was released by the CIA in their document package under the FOIA.</p>
<p>Epstein told the CIA that if search or copying fees did not exceed $5,000, the agency should provide the information without first notifying him of the cost, as he agreed to pay to that amount. He also asked the CIA to identify the specific exemptions supporting any withholding.</p>
<p>On November 5, 1999, the CIA formally accepted the request as P-1999-02450.</p>
<p>The agency told Epstein:</p>
<blockquote><p>&#8220;This acknowledges receipt of your letter dated 28 October 1999 wherein you requested information about yourself.&#8221;</p></blockquote>
<p>The CIA said the request would be processed under both FOIA and the Privacy Act and that its search would cover records existing through November 5, 1999.</p>
<p>The documents then show the request moving through internal CIA search channels.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-07-07.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-21497" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-07-07.jpg" alt="" width="947" height="509" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-07-07.jpg 947w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-07-07-300x161.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-07-07-150x81.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-07-07-450x242.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-07-07-768x413.jpg 768w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-07-07-600x322.jpg 600w" sizes="(max-width: 947px) 100vw, 947px" /></a></p>
<p>One November 1999 internal record identifies the requester as &#8220;EPSTEIN, JEFFREY,&#8221; describes the subject as &#8220;INFO ON SELF,&#8221; and records &#8220;NO RECORD FOUND&#8221;. This document appears to be only one component within the CIA that searched for records, with a second that would take another two years to complete their search.</p>
<p><strong>Five Months Later, Epstein Wants an Answer</strong></p>
<p>By April 2000, Epstein was pressing the CIA for a response.</p>
<p>In an April 7 letter, he wrote that more than five months had passed since his original submission:</p>
<blockquote><p>&#8220;I would appreciate any efforts that could be taken to expedite a response to my request.&#8221;</p></blockquote>
<p>The CIA answered on April 20.</p>
<p>The agency attributed the delay to its workload, telling Epstein that it was handling approximately 5,000 FOIA and Privacy Act requests and processing them on a first-in, first-out basis. It could not provide a definite completion date. Although Epstein was not notified of the initial negative search result at that stage, the case was still being processed and additional searches remained underway. The newly released internal records therefore provide a chronological view of the CIA’s processing of the request, including interim search results that were not separately communicated to Epstein before the agency issued its final determination.</p>
<p>The case would remain unresolved for nearly another two years.</p>
<p>A February 19, 2002, internal CIA memorandum records a search involving the Directorate of Operations.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-01-42.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-21496" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-01-42.jpg" alt="" width="857" height="445" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-01-42.jpg 857w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-01-42-300x156.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-01-42-150x78.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-01-42-450x234.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-01-42-768x399.jpg 768w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-01-42-600x312.jpg 600w" sizes="(max-width: 857px) 100vw, 857px" /></a>Its conclusion was:</p>
<blockquote><p>&#8220;Subject has been searched with all components of this Directorate which might have an interest or record. This Directorate has no information which can be identified with the Subject.&#8221;</p></blockquote>
<p>Two days later, the CIA issued its final answer.</p>
<p>On February 21, 2002, more than two years after Epstein&#8217;s original request, the CIA closed P-1999-02450.</p>
<p>The agency wrote:</p>
<blockquote><p>&#8220;We were unable to identify any information or record filed under your name.&#8221;</p></blockquote>
<p>The CIA characterized its searches as &#8220;thorough and diligent&#8221; and said it was &#8220;highly unlikely&#8221; that repeating them would produce a different result, although Epstein retained the right to appeal.</p>
<p>The newly released records therefore provide considerably more information about the 1999 case than simply its case number. They show what Epstein requested, how portions of the CIA searched for responsive information, his follow-up when the process stalled, and the agency&#8217;s ultimate no-records determination.</p>
<p>But that was not Epstein&#8217;s last request.</p>
<p><strong>A Much More Specific Request in 2011</strong></p>
<p>On June 28, 2011, attorney Martin G. Weinberg submitted another FOIA and Privacy Act request to the CIA on Epstein&#8217;s behalf.</p>
<p>Unlike Epstein&#8217;s brief 1999 request for all records indexed to his name, the 2011 request specified both a time period and particular categories of records.</p>
<p>Weinberg sought records created between January 1, 2000 and June 28, 2011, including records &#8220;prepared, received, transmitted, collected, and/or maintained&#8221; by the CIA or its components and field offices.</p>
<p>The first category sought:</p>
<blockquote><p>&#8220;Any records, order, agreement, or accommodation relating or referring to Epstein, including but not limited to records that document monitoring, surveillance, observation, questioning, interrogation, investigation, and/or collection of information relating to Epstein.&#8221;</p></blockquote>
<p>The second sought:</p>
<blockquote><p>&#8220;Any records relating or referring to how, why, or when Epstein was selected to be a subject of monitoring, surveillance, observation, questioning, interrogation, investigation, and/or collection of information.&#8221;</p></blockquote>
<p>Epstein&#8217;s attorney also specifically requested a &#8220;cross-reference search&#8221; and preauthorized as much as $1,000 in fees.</p>
<p>The wording is noteworthy because it establishes precisely what Epstein wanted the CIA to search for. It does not, by itself, establish that the CIA had monitored, surveilled, questioned, investigated or maintained an intelligence relationship with Epstein, but rather, simply shows what Epstein was seeking.</p>
<p><strong>CIA Requires Proof of Epstein&#8217;s Authorization</strong></p>
<figure id="attachment_21502" aria-describedby="caption-attachment-21502" style="width: 203px" class="wp-caption alignright"><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-26-24.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="size-medium wp-image-21502" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-26-24-203x300.jpg" alt="" width="203" height="300" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-26-24-203x300.jpg 203w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-26-24-150x222.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-26-24-450x665.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-26-24-600x887.jpg 600w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_07-26-24.jpg 613w" sizes="(max-width: 203px) 100vw, 203px" /></a><figcaption id="caption-attachment-21502" class="wp-caption-text">Privacy Waiver signed by Jeffrey Epstein</figcaption></figure>
<p>The CIA assigned the second matter P-2011-00673.</p>
<p>But processing initially stalled because the agency said Weinberg&#8217;s facsimile did not include the privacy waiver referenced in his request.</p>
<p>The CIA explained that it could conduct a search without authorization, but privacy considerations could restrict disclosure if records were found. It therefore required a properly executed statement establishing Epstein&#8217;s identity and authorizing his attorney to receive the results.</p>
<p>The agency gave Weinberg 45 days to provide the documentation.</p>
<p>On July 19, Weinberg sent another letter renewing the request and enclosed what he described as &#8220;an executed privacy waiver and certification of identity for Jeffery Edward Epstein.&#8221;</p>
<p>The release includes that signed authorization. In it, Epstein waived his privacy rights for purposes of the request and authorized the CIA to release information about him to Weinberg.</p>
<p>CIA records also document contact with Weinberg&#8217;s office. A July 20 entry states:</p>
<blockquote><p>&#8220;I spoke with Mr. Weinberg regarding his client Mr. Epstein&#8217;s PA request. I confirmed he did receive our letter and he stated they sent out the Privacy Waiver to us via Fed Ex.&#8221;</p></blockquote>
<p><strong>The CIA Searches Again</strong></p>
<p>The release contains records documenting the CIA&#8217;s second round of searches.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-16-32.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-21498" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-16-32.jpg" alt="" width="936" height="569" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-16-32.jpg 936w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-16-32-300x182.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-16-32-150x91.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-16-32-450x274.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-16-32-768x467.jpg 768w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_06-16-32-600x365.jpg 600w" sizes="(max-width: 936px) 100vw, 936px" /></a></p>
<p>A July 2011 &#8220;NCS Upfront Person-Search Request Form&#8221; states that searches of the Office of Security central database indicated that the Directorate of Support held no record on Epstein and requested that NCS record systems also be checked. The handwritten search result is marked &#8220;Negative.&#8221;</p>
<p>Other released pages show searches of an internal security system using Epstein&#8217;s identifying information. One screen records a search using his date of birth, while another shows a search using his Social Security number.</p>
<p>Then, on July 29, 2011, the CIA issued its final determination.</p>
<p>That response requires careful distinction between two categories of possible records.</p>
<p><strong>No Records of an Open or Acknowledged CIA Affiliation</strong></p>
<p>For records that could reflect an overt or acknowledged connection between Epstein and the agency, the CIA said it found nothing.</p>
<p>The agency wrote:</p>
<blockquote><p>&#8220;We searched for CIA-originated responsive records that might reflect an open or otherwise acknowledged Agency affiliation existing from 5 November 1999, the date of our previous acceptance letter (P-1999-02450) to 25 July 2011, the date we accepted Mr. Epstein&#8217;s current request. We were unable to locate any information or records.&#8221;</p></blockquote>
<p>That statement is the CIA&#8217;s documented search result for an open or otherwise acknowledged agency affiliation during the specified period.</p>
<p>But the next paragraph gave a different answer concerning any potentially classified connection.</p>
<p><strong>CIA Would Neither Confirm Nor Deny a Classified Connection</strong></p>
<p>The CIA wrote:</p>
<blockquote><p>&#8220;With respect to responsive records that would reveal a classified connection to the CIA, in accordance with section 3.6(a) of Executive Order 13526, as amended, the CIA can neither confirm nor deny the existence or nonexistence of records responsive to your request.&#8221;</p></blockquote>
<p>The agency said the existence or nonexistence of such records was classified and constituted intelligence sources-and-methods information protected by federal law. It invoked FOIA Exemptions (b)(1) and (b)(3), along with Privacy Act exemptions (j)(1) and (k)(1).</p>
<p>This is commonly known as a Glomar response: rather than acknowledging that responsive records exist and withholding them, an agency declines to confirm or deny whether the records exist at all.</p>
<p>The language therefore should not be read as confirmation that Epstein had a classified relationship with the CIA. Nor does it establish that no such records existed. The CIA expressly declined to answer that question.</p>
<p><strong>An Earlier Epstein Case Had Already Been Destroyed</strong></p>
<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_08-17-07.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="alignright size-medium wp-image-21505" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_08-17-07-300x203.jpg" alt="" width="300" height="203" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_08-17-07-300x203.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_08-17-07-150x102.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_08-17-07-450x305.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_08-17-07-600x407.jpg 600w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-24_08-17-07.jpg 659w" sizes="(max-width: 300px) 100vw, 300px" /></a>One of the more consequential administrative details appears on the final page of the release.</p>
<p>An internal notation concerning the 2011 determination states:</p>
<p>&#8220;MFR: Previous case destroyed 14 Sept 2005 per NARA destruction schedule&#8221;</p>
<p>The &#8220;previous case&#8221; is identified in the surrounding 2011 records as P-1999-02450. The newly released package nevertheless contains copies of numerous documents associated with that earlier matter, including Epstein&#8217;s original request and the CIA&#8217;s correspondence and search documentation.</p>
<p>The notation establishes that the previous case was recorded as having been destroyed pursuant to a National Archives and Records Administration destruction schedule. The released material does not, by itself, explain precisely which original case materials were destroyed in 2005 or why copies of some of those records remained available elsewhere for the CIA&#8217;s 2026 search.</p>
<p><strong>What the Records Establish — and What They Don&#8217;t</strong></p>
<p>The newly released material establishes a documented chronology.</p>
<p>Epstein asked the CIA in October 1999 for all records about himself indexed to his name. The CIA searched and ultimately told him in February 2002 that it could not identify any such information or records. Internal records released with the case document negative searches, including searches of personnel security systems and the Directorate of Operations.</p>
<p>In 2011, Epstein, through an attorney, returned with a considerably more detailed request. He specifically sought records concerning possible monitoring, surveillance, questioning, investigation or collection of information about him and requested a cross-reference search.</p>
<p>The CIA again reported no records reflecting an open or otherwise acknowledged Agency affiliation between November 5, 1999 and July 25, 2011. For anything that would reveal a classified connection, however, the CIA neither confirmed nor denied the existence of records.</p>
<p>Nothing in the newly released documents establishes that Epstein worked for, assisted, was recruited by, or otherwise maintained a relationship with the CIA. The records establish that Epstein repeatedly asked the CIA what it had on him, and they now reveal substantially more about how the agency processed and answered those requests.</p>
<p>More than two decades after the first case was opened, those administrative files provide a documented answer to at least part of that question: when the CIA searched for records it could acknowledge concerning Epstein, it reported finding none. On the separate question of whether records reflecting a classified connection existed, the agency&#8217;s answer in 2011 was neither yes nor no.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p class="PDq2pG_selectionAnchorContainer" dir="auto" data-start="128" data-end="567"><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-23_05-44-18.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="alignright size-medium wp-image-21490" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-23_05-44-18-300x300.jpg" alt="" width="300" height="300" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-23_05-44-18-300x300.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-23_05-44-18-150x149.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-23_05-44-18-450x448.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-23_05-44-18-600x597.jpg 600w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-23_05-44-18-336x336.jpg 336w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-23_05-44-18.jpg 645w" sizes="(max-width: 300px) 100vw, 300px" /></a>A Freedom of Information Act request seeking National Security Agency records mentioning or cross-referencing QAnon has produced a limited set of material: an internal employee discussion in which QAnon was invoked casually as a joke, records associated with an earlier FOIA request filed by The Black Vault, and, more significantly, confirmation that the NSA located a separate three-page document that remains classified SECRET and was withheld in its entirety.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="569" data-end="866">The NSA&#8217;s March 25, 2026, response concerned a FOIA request filed on February 10, 2020, seeking records that “pertain or mention or contain cross references to QAnon.” The request covered records dating from 2016 through the date the request was processed.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="868" data-end="1306">The response does not disclose the subject, origin, date or contents of the three-page classified record. Consequently, the release provides no basis to conclude what connection the withheld document has to QAnon. It could contain a substantive discussion of the movement, a passing reference, a cross-reference comparable to the material that was released, or something else entirely. The available record does not resolve that question.</p>
<h4 dir="auto" data-section-id="dahj1q" data-start="1308" data-end="1345">A SECRET Document Withheld in Full</h4>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1347" data-end="1775">According to the NSA, “1 document (3 pages) has been withheld in its entirety.” The agency said the document remains properly classified under Executive Order 13526 and specifically cited Sections 1.4(c) and 1.4(g). NSA further stated that the record remains classified at the SECRET level and that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to cause serious damage to the national security.”</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1777" data-end="2322">Section 1.4(c) of Executive Order 13526 covers classified information concerning “intelligence activities (including covert action), intelligence sources or methods, or cryptology.” Section 1.4(g) covers “vulnerabilities or capabilities of systems, installations, infrastructures, projects, plans, or protection services relating to the national security.” Those classification categories establish the grounds cited by NSA for protecting the information; they do not establish what the three-page document says or how QAnon figures into it.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2324" data-end="2661">NSA invoked FOIA Exemption 1, which protects properly classified national-security information. It also invoked Exemption 3 and identified three statutes applicable to the fully withheld document: 18 U.S.C. § 798, 50 U.S.C. § 3024(h), and Section 6 of Public Law 86-36, codified at 50 U.S.C. § 3605.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2663" data-end="2851">NSA additionally stated that it “reasonably foresees” disclosure of the withheld material would harm an interest protected by the exemptions it cited.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2853" data-end="3101">Nothing in the response identifies whether the classified document was created by NSA, originated elsewhere in the Intelligence Community, or merely contains a QAnon reference that caused it to become responsive to the broad wording of the request.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="3103" data-end="3265">That distinction matters because the unclassified record released alongside it demonstrates how little a keyword hit can reveal about a document&#8217;s actual subject.</p>
<h3 dir="auto" data-section-id="1pz46ui" data-start="3267" data-end="3309">The Released QAnon Reference Was a Joke</h3>
<p dir="auto" data-start="3311" data-end="3478">The substantive released material is a November 2019 discussion among NSA personnel concerning personnel policies, field tours, deployments and promotion requirements.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="3480" data-end="3808">The pages are printouts from an NSA system labeled “Tapioca: The Stream.” The interface displays threaded posts and replies among multiple users, along with timestamps and automatically associated “Implicit Topics.” The released thread states that it contains 23 replies from 13 people.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="3810" data-end="4220">The conversation began after a feed apparently cut out while the Director of NSA was discussing field-tour policy. Employees then attempted to reconstruct what had been said, discussing possible requirements for field tours or deployments, promotion to the GS-15 level, jobs at NSA Washington and the meaning of personnel terminology.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4222" data-end="4463">One participant complained that field-tour advertisements seemed to disappear. Another said someone had told them that advertisements were displayed only on certain days of the week before being removed.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4465" data-end="4539">That prompted the sole QAnon reference visible in the released discussion:</p>
<blockquote data-start="4541" data-end="4766">
<p dir="auto" data-start="4543" data-end="4766">“This actually sounds like some qAnon conspiracy theory. Field ads appear in the hr tool just like other jobs. If you want a specific job, you should write to the org and ask about it.”</p>
</blockquote>
<p dir="auto" data-start="4768" data-end="5091">In other words, the released thread is not an NSA analysis of QAnon. The term was used colloquially by an employee during an unrelated workplace discussion. Because the 2020 FOIA request explicitly sought records that merely “mention” or “contain” references to QAnon, that passing reference made the discussion responsive.</p>
<h4 dir="auto" data-section-id="l1vv8o" data-start="5093" data-end="5113">What is Tapioca?</h4>
<p dir="auto" data-start="5115" data-end="5280">The released pages themselves provide useful context for Tapioca, although NSA does not provide a separate technical description of the system in this FOIA response.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="5115" data-end="5280"><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-23_05-16-31.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-21488" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-23_05-16-31.jpg" alt="" width="940" height="363" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-23_05-16-31.jpg 940w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-23_05-16-31-300x116.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-23_05-16-31-150x58.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-23_05-16-31-450x174.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-23_05-16-31-768x297.jpg 768w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-23_05-16-31-600x232.jpg 600w" sizes="(max-width: 940px) 100vw, 940px" /></a></p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="5282" data-end="5673">The records identify the interface as “Tapioca: The Stream” and show a social-media-style internal discussion environment. The pages include tabs labeled “The Main Stream,” “Tributaries,” “Tributary Events” and “Search,” while individual entries contain replies, timestamps, topic tags and links indicating that messages were “posted to The Stream.”</p>
<h4 dir="auto" data-section-id="1870iok" data-start="6109" data-end="6149">An Earlier QAnon Search Found Nothing</h4>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6151" data-end="6431">The 2026 release also contains records from a separate August 2018 FOIA request, originally filed by The Black Vault, seeking Intellipedia entries for “QANON” or “Q-ANON,” including entries returned through an Intellipedia keyword search.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6433" data-end="6915">NSA explained at the time that it serves as executive agent for Intelink and provides technical services enabling Intelligence Community and Department of Defense users to access and share information. It also cautioned that Intellipedia pages are collaborative “living documents” that can be created and edited by users from different organizations and should not be regarded as the final, coordinated position of the Intelligence Community.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="6917" data-end="7076">NSA reported that a “thorough search of Intellipedia files was conducted,” but that no responsive records were located.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="7078" data-end="7338">That August 2018 result and the March 2026 response concern different searches and should not be conflated. The earlier request was specifically directed at Intellipedia. The later request was broader, seeking NSA records containing or cross-referencing QAnon.</p>
<h4 dir="auto" data-section-id="1wc0ir5" data-start="7340" data-end="7390">What QAnon Was — and What Was Never Established</h4>
<p dir="auto" data-start="7392" data-end="7705">QAnon emerged online in 2017 around anonymous posts attributed to a figure calling itself “Q,” who claimed access to sensitive government information. The posts became the foundation for an expansive collection of conspiracy claims involving political figures, government officials and other public personalities.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="7707" data-end="8053">Central QAnon claims, which included allegations of a secret cabal engaged in child trafficking and assertions that hidden government operations were underway against that cabal, were never substantiated by credible evidence. The existence of government records discussing QAnon should likewise not be confused with evidence supporting those claims.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="8055" data-end="8694">Government agencies nevertheless had legitimate reasons to document QAnon as a social and, in some cases, security phenomenon. An FBI record from October 2018, for example, documents database checks and open-source research concerning QAnon. Later FBI and Department of Homeland Security reporting discussed conspiracy theories, including QAnon, in the context of some domestic violent extremists, while emphasizing that ideology or belief alone is not what defines domestic terrorism; the government&#8217;s framework centers on criminal acts, threats or violence.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="8696" data-end="8750">None of that validates QAnon&#8217;s underlying allegations.</p>
<h4 class="PDq2pG_selectionAnchorContainer" dir="auto" data-section-id="g1yxnt" data-start="234" data-end="284">Previous CIA and FBI Responses on QAnon Records</h4>
<p dir="auto" data-start="286" data-end="669">The NSA response is not the first time a federal intelligence or law-enforcement agency has withheld information in response to a QAnon-related FOIA request. Previous requests by The Black Vault produced substantially different responses from the CIA and FBI.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="671" data-end="1149">In February 2020, The Black Vault submitted a request to the CIA for records pertaining to QAnon. The agency responded two days later with what is commonly known as a <a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/cia-glomar-response-on-qanon-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Glomar response</a>: it would neither confirm nor deny the existence or nonexistence of responsive records. According to the CIA response as reported by The Black Vault at the time, even acknowledging whether such records existed would itself reveal classified information.</p>
<p class="" dir="auto" data-start="1151" data-end="1581">The FBI later took a different approach. In records released to The Black Vault and reported in May 2023, the Bureau acknowledged <a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/fbi-releases-files-on-qanon-withholds-more-than-half-of-them/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">locating 43 pages</a> related to QAnon but released only 19 pages. The remaining pages were withheld under FOIA Exemption 7(A), which applies to law-enforcement records when disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.”</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="1583" data-end="2050">The newly released NSA response adds another, distinct piece to that record. Unlike the CIA, NSA has now acknowledged that responsive records exist. And unlike the FBI withholding, which was based on interference with enforcement proceedings, NSA identified a separate three-page document as currently classified SECRET, stating that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to cause serious damage to the national security.”</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="2052" data-end="2673" data-is-last-node="" data-is-only-node="">Taken together, the three responses establish only what each agency has formally acknowledged through FOIA: the CIA refused in 2020 to say whether it possessed responsive records; the FBI later acknowledged 43 pages while withholding more than half of them on law-enforcement grounds; and NSA has now acknowledged responsive material that includes a three-page SECRET document withheld in full. They do not establish that the CIA, FBI and NSA records are connected, concern the same underlying matter, or substantiate any of QAnon’s claims.</p>
<h4 dir="auto" data-section-id="1jyhrk2" data-start="8752" data-end="8807">What the NSA Release Does — and Does Not — Establish</h4>
<p dir="auto" data-start="8809" data-end="9111">The March 2026 FOIA response establishes a narrow but potentially significant fact: when NSA processed a broad request for records pertaining to, mentioning, containing or cross-referencing QAnon, it identified a three-page document that it determined remains classified SECRET and withheld every page.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="9113" data-end="9172">It does not establish why that document was responsive.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="9174" data-end="9551">NSA&#8217;s decision to withhold it under national-security and statutory exemptions reveals classification grounds, but not the nature of the QAnon connection. The released Tapioca thread provides a useful cautionary example: a record can qualify as responsive simply because someone mentioned “qAnon conspiracy theory” in an otherwise unrelated conversation about personnel policy.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="9553" data-end="9819">The three-page SECRET record could similarly contain only an incidental reference, or its connection to QAnon could be more substantive. With all three pages withheld and no description of their contents provided, the public record does not permit either conclusion.</p>
<p dir="auto" data-start="9821" data-end="9937" data-is-last-node="" data-is-only-node="">For now, what the document contains, and why the term QAnon led NSA to identify it as responsive,  remains unknown.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-47-50.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="alignright size-medium wp-image-21482" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-47-50-300x214.jpg" alt="" width="300" height="214" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-47-50-300x214.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-47-50-150x107.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-47-50-450x322.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-47-50-600x429.jpg 600w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-47-50.jpg 669w" sizes="(max-width: 300px) 100vw, 300px" /></a>A mathematical study published inside the National Security Agency six decades ago has been released through the Freedom of Information Act, more than nine years after The Black Vault requested it.</p>
<p>The paper, “Coupon Collecting and Cryptology,” was written by Marie Capozza and published in the Fall 1966 edition of the NSA Technical Journal. Although the title had been publicly identified for years in declassified indexes of NSA publications, searches conducted by The Black Vault did not locate a previously public copy of the paper itself. The 1966 study released in response to the FOIA request therefore provides a newly available look at how NSA mathematicians examined a classic probability problem and applied it to cryptologic questions.</p>
<p>The Black Vault requested the article from NSA on August 6, 2017. NSA&#8217;s response is dated September 9, 2026, more than nine years later. The agency released the responsive document with portions withheld under FOIA Exemptions 1 and 3.</p>
<p>Despite its complex pages of equations, the basic idea behind the paper is relatively straightforward: If items are selected randomly from a fixed number of possible types, how long should it take before every type has appeared; and can deviations from that expected behavior reveal that the selection process is not actually random?</p>
<p>For the NSA, that question had direct applications to cryptology.</p>
<p><strong>From Cereal-Box Coupons to Cryptology</strong></p>
<p>Capozza begins with a deliberately ordinary example.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-55-43.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="size-medium wp-image-21483 alignleft" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-55-43-300x298.jpg" alt="" width="300" height="298" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-55-43-300x298.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-55-43-150x149.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-55-43-450x447.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-55-43-600x596.jpg 600w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-55-43.jpg 652w" sizes="(max-width: 300px) 100vw, 300px" /></a>“Let us assume that a manufacturer encloses in each package of his product one coupon that is chosen randomly from a stock of K different coupons.”</p>
<p>The manufacturer might offer a prize to someone who obtains every different coupon. The mathematical problem is determining how many packages, on average, a customer would have to purchase before completing the set.</p>
<p>That is what mathematicians call the coupon collector&#8217;s problem.</p>
<p>Capozza describes the purpose of the paper as presenting “some well-known and some new results found in the literature on coupon collecting and to point out some cryptologic applications.”</p>
<p>In plain English, imagine that there are ten different coupons and every box contains one, selected independently and with equal probability. Finding the first several different coupons is easy. The problem becomes progressively harder as the collection approaches completion. Once nine different coupons have been obtained, every new box has only a one-in-ten chance of containing the single missing coupon.</p>
<p>That last stubborn item substantially increases the average waiting time.</p>
<p>Capozza calculates that when there are ten possible coupons, the expected number of selections required to collect all ten is approximately 29.29. The paper notes:</p>
<blockquote><p>“Suppose that we have decimal digits. Then the expected number of drawings to exhaust the entire population … [is]29.29.”</p></blockquote>
<p>The same mathematics applies to an alphabet. For 26 equally probable letters, the paper calculates an expected collection time of approximately 100.20 selections before all 26 have appeared.</p>
<p>These examples provide the foundation for the paper&#8217;s more consequential subject: testing whether sequences used in cryptographic systems behave as though they are genuinely random.</p>
<p><strong>Why Randomness Matters</strong></p>
<p>Cryptographic systems depend heavily on sequences that cannot be readily predicted. A sequence may look chaotic to a person while still containing mathematical biases or patterns.</p>
<p>The coupon collector framework offers one way of testing those sequences.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-59-52.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="size-medium wp-image-21484 alignright" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-59-52-300x300.jpg" alt="" width="300" height="300" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-59-52-300x300.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-59-52-150x150.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-59-52-450x449.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-59-52-600x599.jpg 600w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-59-52-336x336.jpg 336w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_09-59-52.jpg 648w" sizes="(max-width: 300px) 100vw, 300px" /></a>Instead of asking how long it takes to collect every coupon, an analyst can ask how many generated symbols must be examined before every possible symbol has occurred. If that process repeatedly takes substantially more or fewer observations than mathematics predicts for genuinely random selections, something about the underlying generator may warrant scrutiny.</p>
<p>Capozza devotes much of the paper to deriving the probability distributions necessary to make those comparisons.</p>
<p>The equations determine, among other things, the probability of obtaining a particular number of distinct coupon types after a given number of selections, the average waiting time required to obtain every possible type, the variance around that average and the limiting behavior of the distribution when the number of possible symbols becomes very large.</p>
<p>One table illustrates how quickly the waiting time grows. For ten possible coupon types, the expected collection time is about 29.29 selections. For 20 types, it is about 71.95. For 30, approximately 119.85. At 40, the expected number rises to approximately 171.16, and for 50 possible types it reaches about 225.06.</p>
<p>The point is not that cryptographers were literally collecting coupons. The coupon story provides a convenient mathematical model for analyzing symbols drawn from a finite alphabet.</p>
<p><strong>Testing Random Numbers</strong></p>
<p>The paper eventually turns explicitly to what Capozza calls “Cryptologic Applications.”</p>
<p>One is a test of randomness.</p>
<p>Capozza describes the “Coupon Collector&#8217;s Test for Randomness” and applies the concept to decimal digits. Under the assumption that digits are random, analysts can calculate how frequently they should need a particular number of observations before seeing all ten digits.</p>
<p>The paper states:</p>
<blockquote><p>“Using the 2035 decimal approximations to π given by G. W. Reitwiesner, Greenwood began with the initial position 3 and found that 33 positions were required to collect all ten digits.”</p></blockquote>
<p>The procedure was then repeated through the sequence, producing 87 sequences of complete sets, according to the study.</p>
<p>Those observed results could be compared with the mathematically expected distribution.</p>
<p>This is an important distinction: the test does not prove that a sequence is random. Rather, it provides a statistical means of identifying behavior that is inconsistent with what would ordinarily be expected from random sampling.</p>
<p>The underlying technique was not unique to NSA. Robert E. Greenwood published <a href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/2002211" target="_blank" rel="noopener">“Coupon Collector&#8217;s Test for Random Digits”</a> in Mathematical Tables and Other Aids to Computation in 1955, and the technique subsequently appeared in the literature on random-number testing.</p>
<p>Capozza&#8217;s NSA paper assembled that mathematical foundation and examined how it could be used in a cryptologic environment.</p>
<p><strong>Generating Cryptographic Alphabets</strong></p>
<p>A second application gets closer to operational cryptography.</p>
<p>Under the heading “Coupon Collecting and Key Card Generation,” Capozza describes a method in which randomly generated coordinates are used to place punches in a 12-by-12 field on a key card.</p>
<p>The goal was to populate the card while avoiding duplicate locations.</p>
<p>The document explains:</p>
<blockquote><p>“If it takes too many trials to complete our matrix, then our ‘random’ process of position selecting is not truly random since it has a tendency to select one or more positions.”</p></blockquote>
<p>Conversely, the study says that completing the matrix with unusually few selections could indicate another form of non-random behavior.</p>
<p>This is the practical significance of the otherwise abstract mathematics.</p>
<p>A random-number source might produce values that look unpredictable when examined casually. But if certain positions or symbols occur too frequently — or others appear too infrequently — coupon-collector statistics can expose that imbalance.</p>
<p>In cryptography, such bias can matter because the security of a system may depend on assumptions about the randomness of its keys or other generated values.</p>
<p><strong>A Second Statistical Test</strong></p>
<p>The paper also discusses what it calls a “Statistical Inference: Coupon Collector&#8217;s Test for the Number of Code Groups.”</p>
<p>Capozza asks analysts to imagine that there are K possible code groups, but that K itself is unknown. After observing a sample containing a certain number of different groups, the analyst can attempt to estimate the total number of groups that exist.</p>
<p>That reverses the original coupon problem.</p>
<p>Instead of knowing how many coupon types exist and calculating how long collecting them should take, the analyst observes repeated samples and attempts to infer the size of the unseen population.</p>
<p>Capozza notes that the underlying population is assumed to be flat — meaning the individual possibilities are treated as equally likely — an assumption that the paper explicitly acknowledges “is not easily satisfied.”</p>
<p>That caveat is important. The mathematics depends upon assumptions about how the underlying population behaves. If those assumptions are wrong, the resulting statistical conclusions may not accurately describe the real system.</p>
<p><strong>A Paper Marked SECRET</strong></p>
<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_10-07-29.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-21485" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_10-07-29.jpg" alt="" width="660" height="289" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_10-07-29.jpg 660w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_10-07-29-300x131.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_10-07-29-150x66.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_10-07-29-450x197.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-22_10-07-29-600x263.jpg 600w" sizes="(max-width: 660px) 100vw, 660px" /></a></p>
<p>Every page of the released article carries SECRET classification markings at the top and bottom. The released copy identifies it as Doc ID 6932166 and Doc Ref ID A3912410.</p>
<p>The first page now also bears a release notation:</p>
<p>“Approved for Release by NSA on 09-09-2026, FOIA Case # 102241.”</p>
<p>NSA did not release everything.</p>
<p>Its September 9 response states that portions remain classified under Executive Order 13526 and were withheld pursuant to FOIA Exemption 1. NSA said disclosure of the classified material “could reasonably be expected to cause serious damage to national security.”</p>
<p>The agency also invoked FOIA Exemption 3, citing statutes protecting intelligence-related information, including 50 U.S.C. §3024(i) and Section 6 of Public Law 86-36. NSA characterized the release as a partial denial of The Black Vault&#8217;s request.</p>
<p>Several portions of the article remain visibly blank or redacted. Most notably, a large section on page 115 under the heading “Coupon Collecting and Alphabet Generation” is withheld. Portions of the bibliography are also redacted.</p>
<p>The contents of those withheld passages cannot be established from the released record.</p>
<p><strong>What the 1966 Study Means in Plain English</strong></p>
<p>Stripped of its equations, Coupon Collecting and Cryptology addresses a fundamental problem: How can mathematics tell whether something that is supposed to be random is behaving the way randomness says it should?</p>
<p>The coupon analogy provides an intuitive way to answer it.</p>
<p>If a bag contains ten equally likely numbered tokens, repeated draws should eventually produce all ten. Mathematics can predict approximately how long that should take and how much variation around that average is normal.</p>
<p>If repeated experiments behave very differently from those predictions, the discrepancy becomes measurable.</p>
<p>Capozza&#8217;s paper develops that concept mathematically and then applies it to cryptologic problems involving random digits, code groups, key-card generation and alphabets.</p>
<p>The advanced formulas filling much of the 13-page article are therefore not describing an exotic cryptographic cipher. They are establishing the probability distributions required to distinguish expected random behavior from potentially significant statistical irregularities.</p>
<p>Sixty years after the study appeared inside the NSA Technical Journal — and more than nine years after The Black Vault asked NSA for a copy — the released portions of Coupon Collecting and Cryptology provide a window into how the agency applied classical probability theory to practical cryptologic problems.</p>
<p>The release also leaves part of that history unresolved. Portions of the 1966 paper remain withheld, including material in its discussion of alphabet generation, meaning the complete scope of the study remains unavailable in the public version released by NSA.</p>

<p>NSA’s response, dated August 29, 2026, came more than 15 years after the request was filed. The agency stated that some responsive documents were being released with information withheld and that one 228-page document was withheld in its entirety. NSA did not identify that document by title.</p>
<p>According to the response, the 228-page document remained “currently and properly classified” under Executive Order 13526, resulting in its withholding under FOIA Exemption 1. NSA also cited Exemption 3 for information protected by federal statutes, specifically Title 18 U.S.C. §798, Title 50 U.S.C. §3024(i), and Section 6 of Public Law 86-36. Personal information was withheld under Exemption 6. The agency further stated that the Department of Energy requested withholding of certain information under Exemptions 6 and 7, with those portions identified as “OGA,” or Other Government Agency.</p>
<p>The eight studies now archived by The Black Vault correspond to eight of the ten titles specifically identified in the 2011 FOIA request. The two requested titles not represented in the released collection are Bio-Inspired Computing and Position Location. The available FOIA response does not identify which requested title corresponds to the 228-page document withheld in full, so it cannot be established from the records whether that document was Bio-Inspired Computing, Position Location, or both. Accordingly, the record establishes only that ten titles were requested, eight are represented in this release, and one unidentified 228-page responsive document was withheld in its entirety.</p>
<p><strong>A Window Into NSA Technology Forecasting</strong></p>
<p>Although the subjects vary considerably, the documents belong together for a specific reason: they were produced as forward-looking assessments intended to examine technologies that could affect national-security missions.</p>
<p>One of the earliest records in the collection, the 2002 Privacy and Anonymity study, provides unusually direct context for that effort. Its preface states that NSA’s Technology Forecasting Program was established to “anticipate the impact of advancing technologies on NSA’s mission.” It describes a process under which analysts examined emerging technologies, market trends and research activity to identify technologies that could affect future operations.</p>
<p>Later reports show national laboratories playing a significant role in that process. Several were produced by Sandia National Laboratories for NSA’s Research Transformation activities, while others were prepared by Pacific Northwest National Laboratory. The documents often combine scientific literature reviews, technology assessments, commercial-market analysis, international research comparisons and forecasts of capabilities several years into the future.</p>
<p>What emerges is not a single research program devoted to one technology. Instead, the papers provide snapshots of what government technology analysts considered potentially consequential at different points between 2002 and 2010: how humans might interact directly with networks, how tiny autonomous devices could power themselves, whether visible light could carry wireless data, what might replace traditional semiconductor scaling, how terahertz systems could be miniaturized, and how advances in antennas could reshape sensing and communications.</p>
<p>Below are the eight released records.</p>
<h3>The Released Documents</h3>
<p><img decoding="async" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/images/pdf.gif" alt="PDF" /> <a href="https://documents3.theblackvault.com/documents/nsa/privacyandanonymity.pdf">Privacy and Anonymity: A Technology Forecast, 29 October 2002</a> [44 Pages, 11.671MB]</p>
<p>This NSA Information Assurance Research Group forecast examines privacy and anonymity at a moment when Internet use, electronic commerce, digital communications and government surveillance were rapidly expanding. Rather than treating privacy and anonymity as technologies themselves, the report examines them as rights and policy concerns being reshaped by technology. Written shortly after the September 11 attacks, it places particular emphasis on the tension between personal privacy and national security. The document reviews U.S. and European privacy law and discusses technologies with significant privacy implications, including Wi-Fi, Secure Socket Layer, public-key infrastructure, virtual private networks, intrusion-detection systems, biometrics, Microsoft&#8217;s Passport and Palladium initiatives, anonymous remailers and Internet anonymizers.</p>
<p>The report also addresses the issue specifically from NSA&#8217;s perspective. It acknowledges a fundamental tension between protecting privacy and anonymity and conducting intelligence operations intended to penetrate those protections when directed against authorized foreign targets. The forecast concludes that technological solutions alone cannot resolve privacy concerns and that legal and policy protections will remain important. It also anticipates that improvements in privacy- and anonymity-enhancing technologies could make NSA&#8217;s intelligence mission &#8220;more challenging and expensive,&#8221; while potentially assisting—or complicating—the Agency&#8217;s information-assurance responsibilities.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/images/pdf.gif" alt="PDF" /> <a href="https://documents3.theblackvault.com/documents/nsa/whatsbeyondmooreslaw.pdf">What’s Beyond Moore’s Law: Emerging Research Materials, May 2009</a> [91 Pages, 26.229MB]</p>
<p>This technical assessment examines what might happen as conventional complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) scaling approaches physical and engineering limits. Rather than assuming that increasing transistor density could continue indefinitely through conventional silicon technology, the report evaluates emerging materials and fabrication techniques that might sustain the historical trajectory associated with Moore&#8217;s Law. Topics include advanced lithography, graphene, carbon nanotubes, nanowires, macromolecules, directed self-assembly, spin materials, complex metal oxides, interfaces and heterointerfaces, along with computational modeling and simulation. Its stated objective is to forecast the sustainability of Moore&#8217;s Law through 2030 and beyond.</p>
<p>The report develops three broad scenarios: carbon-based nanoelectronics replacing or supplementing silicon; complex metal oxides assuming a larger role in future devices; or silicon remaining dominant while the historical rate of Moore&#8217;s Law gradually declines. Its forecast anticipated a difficult transitional period as conventional CMOS scaling weakened before replacement technologies became sufficiently mature. The report identified carbon-based nanoelectronics—particularly graphene and carbon nanotubes—as an important potential route beyond conventional silicon, while explicitly recognizing major unresolved technical problems. Its long-range projections, including terahertz-range single-core performance and trillions of transistors per chip by around 2030, are forecasts made from the technological perspective of the report&#8217;s period rather than statements of present-day capability.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/images/pdf.gif" alt="PDF" /> <a href="https://documents3.theblackvault.com/documents/nsa/terahertzsolidstatemicroelectronics.pdf">Technology Point Forecast: Terahertz Solid-State Microelectronics, April 2009</a> [109 Pages, 22.913MB]</p>
<p>Prepared by Sandia National Laboratories, this report assesses efforts to develop practical solid-state electronic components operating in the terahertz portion of the electromagnetic spectrum, which it generally defines for its purposes as approximately 0.5 to 5 THz. The document describes terahertz frequencies as occupying a technological gap between microwave electronics and infrared photonics: conventional transistor-based electronics become increasingly difficult at the upper end, while conventional photonic technologies encounter limitations approaching from higher frequencies. The forecast evaluates research aimed at creating compact, manufacturable solid-state sources and detectors capable of closing that gap.</p>
<p>The report contrasts this goal with existing tube-based terahertz sources, which could provide useful performance but carried substantial disadvantages in size, weight, operating voltage, power requirements and mechanical complexity. It cites, for example, a 2.5-THz molecular gas laser developed for NASA&#8217;s Aura satellite as evidence that rugged terahertz systems were possible, while noting the substantial engineering expense involved. The forecast therefore focuses on solid-state approaches and assesses competing research directions, technical hurdles, anticipated component requirements and leading research groups worldwide, with an eye toward applications relevant to national security, defense and intelligence.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/images/pdf.gif" alt="PDF" /> <a href="https://documents3.theblackvault.com/documents/nsa/overviewofharvestedpower.pdf">Overview of Harvested Power and the Need, April 2009</a> [37 Pages, 12.255MB]</p>
<p>This Sandia National Laboratories report examines &#8220;energy harvesting&#8221; as an alternative to relying exclusively on stored battery power for miniature sensors, actuators and communications equipment. The report explains that shrinking conventional batteries can sharply reduce practical energy density because packaging, seals and other inactive components occupy an increasing proportion of the available volume. It therefore evaluates methods for collecting energy from the surrounding environment, including photovoltaic, chemical, thermal, mechanical and radio-frequency sources. The document compares the energy densities, conversion efficiencies and practical limitations associated with each approach.</p>
<p>The forecast goes beyond individual energy-harvesting devices by examining the supporting systems required to make them useful. Because harvesters frequently cannot meet the instantaneous power requirements of electronics, the report discusses batteries, supercapacitors and power-management circuitry as load-leveling mechanisms. It also provides short-, medium- and long-term capability forecasts, including the possibility that advances in antennas, rectifiers and low-power electronics could eventually make RF energy harvesting practical across substantial portions of urban environments. The broader forecast is that harvested power would become increasingly important as electronic systems became smaller, more autonomous and expected to operate for longer periods without conventional battery replacement.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/images/pdf.gif" alt="PDF" /> <a href="https://documents3.theblackvault.com/documents/nsa/pointforecast.pdf">Point Forecast for Small Energy Storage and Ultrathin Electronics, March 2010</a> [59 Pages, 18.380MB]</p>
<p>Prepared by Sandia National Laboratories for NSA Research Transformation, this forecast examines technologies capable of supplying power to increasingly small electronic devices while simultaneously reducing the physical thickness of the electronics themselves. The report surveys ultrathin and printable batteries, battery materials, supercapacitors, mechanical energy-storage systems, radioisotopic power systems and ultrathin semiconductors. Its central concern is the engineering problem created when sensors, communications devices and other electronics become so small that conventional batteries and packaging consume a disproportionate amount of their available volume.</p>
<p>The forecast also tracks international research activity, including work in China, Taiwan, Japan, South Korea, Russia, Australia, France and the United Kingdom. On the electronics side, it examines efforts to thin conventional silicon circuits into flexible structures approximately 5 to 50 micrometers thick, as well as organic semiconductor alternatives. The document connects these advances to applications such as smart cards, wearable sensors and low-profile communications equipment, while emphasizing that reliability, heat dissipation, packaging and energy density remained significant constraints.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/images/pdf.gif" alt="PDF" /> <a href="https://documents3.theblackvault.com/documents/nsa/forecastofantennatechnologies.pdf">Forecasting of Antenna Technologies, March 2010</a> [38 Pages, 10.651MB]</p>
<p>This Sandia National Laboratories forecast, sponsored by NSA Research Transformation, examines emerging antenna technologies needed to support increasingly capable and miniaturized wireless systems. It concentrates on four principal areas: electrically small antennas, metamaterial-based antennas, advanced antenna-array architectures and new approaches to integrating materials into antenna systems. The report argues that antennas should no longer be treated as an afterthought in system design, particularly as wireless devices are required to support multiple functions while meeting increasingly stringent size, power and performance requirements.</p>
<p>The study evaluates electrically small antennas for applications such as RFID and communications, metamaterials intended to alter or improve electromagnetic behavior, smart and phased arrays, and flexible or mechanically reconfigurable antenna concepts. It also surveys international research and publication activity, including Chinese work in areas such as monopulse systems, RF stealth and radar-absorbing materials. The forecast concludes with short-, medium- and long-term assessments of technical readiness, framing antenna development as an enabling technology for future communications, sensing and other wireless systems rather than merely a peripheral hardware component.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/images/pdf.gif" alt="PDF" /> <a href="https://documents3.theblackvault.com/documents/nsa/usingsolidstatelighting.pdf">Technology Forecast: Using Solid-State Lighting for Wireless Communications</a>, February 2010 [61 Pages, 15.479MB]</p>
<p>Prepared by Pacific Northwest National Laboratory, this forecast examines whether light-emitting diode solid-state lighting could simultaneously illuminate spaces and function as a wireless communications infrastructure. The underlying concept is visible-light communication: because LED output can be modulated rapidly, lighting fixtures could potentially transmit digital information while continuing to provide apparently continuous illumination to occupants. The report considers both indoor networking and point-to-point optical communications and reviews the rapid development of high-brightness LEDs that was making solid-state lighting increasingly practical.</p>
<p>The report tempers some of the more ambitious expectations surrounding the technology. Earlier projections had contemplated gigabit-per-second data rates and communication over hundreds of meters, but experimental systems, physical modeling and practical engineering constraints had caused those expectations to be revised downward by the time of the study. The forecast evaluates LED performance, modulation, receivers, networking architectures and the relationship between lighting requirements and communications performance. In effect, it examines an early version of what later became widely known as visible-light communications or &#8220;Li-Fi&#8221;: using existing or future LED lighting infrastructure as an additional medium for transmitting information.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/images/pdf.gif" alt="PDF" /> <a href="https://documents3.theblackvault.com/documents/nsa/humansideofthenet.pdf">The Human Side of the Net, March 2010</a> [99 Pages, 27.450MB]</p>
<p>Prepared by Sandia National Laboratories, The Human Side of the Net explores neuroscience and neurotechnology as potential components of future computer networks. The report surveys technologies including EEG, fMRI, MEG, PET, functional near-infrared spectroscopy, transcranial stimulation and other methods for measuring or influencing brain activity. Its central forecasting question is unusually direct: how might humans become &#8220;more than metaphorically&#8221; part of a network? From that premise, the study examines the possibility of direct input from the brain to a network, biometric encryption and identification based on unique neurological signals, cognitive-state detection, deception detection and direct control of devices.</p>
<p>The report then considers more speculative applications that researchers might eventually pursue if neurotechnology improved sufficiently. These include using the brain as a supplementary processor, &#8220;brain onion routing,&#8221; outsourcing recognition tasks to human participants, and establishing two-way interaction between the brain and a computer network. Importantly, these concepts are presented as forecast scenarios rather than evidence that NSA possessed or deployed such capabilities. Large portions of the document are redacted under FOIA exemptions, including material withheld under Exemption 7(E), leaving some of the original analysis unavailable in the released version.</p><p>The post <a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/nsa-releases-cold-war-era-style-technology-forecasts-on-brain-network-interfaces-terahertz-systems-energy-harvesting-and-more/">NSA Releases Cold War-Era Style “Technology Forecasts” on Brain-Network Interfaces, Terahertz Systems, Energy Harvesting and More</a> first appeared on <a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive">The Black Vault</a>.</p>]]></content:encoded>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p>The National Security Agency has upheld its refusal to confirm or deny whether it possesses intelligence records concerning the alleged “Immaculate Constellation” program, rejecting a Freedom of Information Act appeal filed by The Black Vault.</p>
<p>The decision does not establish that Immaculate Constellation exists, that NSA participated in such a program, or even that NSA possesses records about it. Instead, NSA maintains that simply answering the question on whether responsive intelligence records exist at all, would itself disclose classified and statutorily protected information.</p>
<p>That distinction is particularly significant in this case. The Department of War <a href="https://archive.dni.gov/files/documents/FOIA/DF-2025-00021-Immaculate-Constellation-descrp-from-UNCLASS-Press-22-Oct-2024.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">has publicly denied</a> having any record of a Special Access Program by that name, while the document responsible for many of the allegations remains an <a href="https://www.congress.gov/118/meeting/house/117721/documents/HHRG-118-GO12-20241113-SD003.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">unverified whistleblower-origin report</a> containing extraordinary claims for which no underlying imagery, intelligence reports, databases or other corroborating evidence described in the report has been publicly produced.</p>
<p>The result is an unusual information gap: one part of the government has publicly said it has no record of the alleged program, while NSA says national security prevents it from saying whether it has records pertaining to the same allegation.</p>
<p><strong>The Immaculate Constellation Allegations</strong></p>
<p>“Immaculate Constellation” entered the public UAP debate in October 2024 through <a href="https://docs.house.gov/meetings/GO/GO12/20241113/117721/HHRG-118-GO12-Wstate-ShellenbergerM-20241113.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">reporting by</a> journalist <a href="https://x.com/shellenberger?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Michael Shellenberger</a>. A document subsequently provided to Congress was entered into the record of the House Oversight Committee&#8217;s November 13, 2024 hearing, “Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena: Exposing the Truth.”</p>
<p>The document describes itself as the product of “a multi-year, internal investigation” concerning UAP, “Technologies of Unknown Origin,” and “Non-Human Intelligence.” It states that it was provided to Congress through UAP whistleblower mechanisms and says its public version was reviewed and approved for public release by the State Department&#8217;s Bureau of Global Public Affairs.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_13-44-36.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-21461" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_13-44-36.jpg" alt="" width="851" height="273" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_13-44-36.jpg 851w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_13-44-36-300x96.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_13-44-36-150x48.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_13-44-36-450x144.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_13-44-36-768x246.jpg 768w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_13-44-36-600x192.jpg 600w" sizes="(max-width: 851px) 100vw, 851px" /></a></p>
<p>Its central allegation is explicit:</p>
<p>“IMMACULATE CONSTELLATION is an Unacknowledged Special Access Program (USAP) established following the public disclosure of the AATIP/AAWSAP programs by Luis Elizondo in 2017.”</p>
<p>The report further alleges that the program&#8217;s “primary mission is collecting imagery intelligence on Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) and ARV/RV (Reproduction Vehicles)” using tasked and untasked U.S. military-intelligence collection systems.</p>
<p>The claims extend considerably further. The document describes alleged imagery from CENTCOM, INDOPACOM, NORTHCOM and SOUTHCOM environments; asserts that a Defense HUMINT database contains more than 400 UAP/UFO encounter reports from 1991 through 2022; and presents tables cataloging reported shapes, sizes, behaviors, signatures and other characteristics.</p>
<p>Those are allegations contained in the document and are not independently established findings.</p>
<p>The document&#8217;s presence in an official congressional hearing record verifies that Congress received and published the report. It does not authenticate the underlying claims, establish that the purported program exists, or demonstrate that the described imagery and databases are genuine.</p>
<p>That evidentiary distinction remains an important part of this. No primary program documentation establishing Immaculate Constellation as an actual USAP has been publicly produced alongside the report, and the underlying imagery and intelligence reporting described in it have not been made publicly available for independent examination.</p>
<p><strong>Pentagon: No Record of the Program</strong></p>
<figure id="attachment_21462" aria-describedby="caption-attachment-21462" style="width: 207px" class="wp-caption alignright"><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_13-47-49.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="wp-image-21462 size-medium" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_13-47-49-207x300.jpg" alt="" width="207" height="300" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_13-47-49-207x300.jpg 207w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_13-47-49-150x218.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_13-47-49-450x653.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_13-47-49.jpg 524w" sizes="(max-width: 207px) 100vw, 207px" /></a><figcaption id="caption-attachment-21462" class="wp-caption-text">Source: FOIA Case DF-2025-00021 (<a href="https://archive.dni.gov/files/documents/FOIA/-Immaculate-Constellation-descrp-from-UNCLASS-Press-22-Oct-2024.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">https://archive.dni.gov/files/documents/FOIA/-Immaculate-Constellation-descrp-from-UNCLASS-Press-22-Oct-2024.pdf</a>)</figcaption></figure>
<p>The Department of Defense publicly took a substantially less ambiguous position.</p>
<p>DoD spokesperson Sue Gough stated:</p>
<p>“The Department of Defense has no record, present or historical, of any type of SAP called ‘IMMACULATE CONSTELLATION.’”</p>
<p>That denial was also recorded in an internal Office of the Director of National Intelligence document concerning the allegations. The ODNI document characterized Immaculate Constellation as a “supposed unacknowledged Special Access Program” and summarized what had been reported publicly before recording Gough&#8217;s denial.</p>
<p>The available public record therefore contains a direct conflict between the whistleblower-origin allegations and DoD&#8217;s stated position. There is currently no publicly available documentary evidence sufficient to independently resolve that conflict in favor of the program&#8217;s existence.</p>
<p><strong>The NSA FOIA Request</strong></p>
<p>On December 3, 2024, The Black Vault submitted a request to NSA seeking intelligence records and records concerning intelligence activities pertaining to the alleged Immaculate Constellation program.</p>
<p>The purpose of filing with NSA was not based on evidence establishing that NSA operated the alleged program. Rather, it was part of a broader FOIA strategy used by The Black Vault when attempting to trace claims involving the Intelligence Community.</p>
<p>Records about a program or subject do not necessarily reside exclusively with the agency alleged to have originated them. Intelligence reporting, correspondence, referrals, finished products or other material can potentially exist elsewhere in government. When responsive records originated with another agency, the FOIA process can also involve consultation or referral to the originating agency for review.</p>
<p>Submitting requests to multiple potentially relevant agencies can therefore test a broader portion of the government&#8217;s documentary record rather than assuming that only one agency could possess responsive material.</p>
<p>NSA answered on December 6, 2024.</p>
<p>But it did not say that it found nothing.</p>
<p>It would not say whether it found anything at all.</p>
<p><strong>NSA Issues a Glomar Response</strong></p>
<figure id="attachment_21463" aria-describedby="caption-attachment-21463" style="width: 225px" class="wp-caption alignleft"><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/GeXY2X8WMAAxlR-.png"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="wp-image-21463 size-medium" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/GeXY2X8WMAAxlR--225x300.jpg" alt="" width="225" height="300" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/GeXY2X8WMAAxlR--225x300.jpg 225w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/GeXY2X8WMAAxlR--150x200.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/GeXY2X8WMAAxlR--450x599.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/GeXY2X8WMAAxlR--600x799.jpg 600w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/GeXY2X8WMAAxlR-.jpg 651w" sizes="(max-width: 225px) 100vw, 225px" /></a><figcaption id="caption-attachment-21463" class="wp-caption-text">NSA denial letter</figcaption></figure>
<p>NSA told The Black Vault:</p>
<p>“For reasons described below, we are not able to confirm or deny the existence or nonexistence of these records.”</p>
<p>Importantly, NSA also explained that its response was not unique to Immaculate Constellation:</p>
<p>“Please be advised that this is our standard response to all requests where we reasonably believe that the request seeks intelligence records or records revealing intelligence related activity involving UFOs/UAP.”</p>
<p>NSA invoked FOIA Exemptions 1 and 3. Under Exemption 1, the agency said that even “the fact of the existence or non-existence” of the requested material was currently and properly classified under Executive Order 13526.</p>
<p>For Exemption 3, NSA cited 18 U.S.C. § 798, 50 U.S.C. § 3024(i), and Section 6 of Public Law 86-36, codified at 50 U.S.C. § 3605.</p>
<p>NSA further stated:</p>
<p>“the Agency reasonably foresees that disclosure of the existence or non-existence of the requested information would be harmful to an interest that is protected by the identified exemptions.”</p>
<p>The response is commonly known as a Glomar response: rather than acknowledging whether responsive records exist and then withholding some or all of them, an agency refuses to confirm or deny the existence of responsive records.</p>
<p>Nothing in NSA&#8217;s December 2024 response can properly be treated as confirmation of Immaculate Constellation. NSA specifically said the response was its standard treatment for requests it believes seek intelligence records or intelligence-related activity involving UFOs or UAP.</p>
<p><strong>The Black Vault Appeals</strong></p>
<p>The Black Vault appealed the decision on December 9, 2024.</p>
<p>The appeal centered in part on the apparent tension between NSA&#8217;s position and the categorical public statement already made by DoD.</p>
<p>It argued that if the alleged program did not exist, acknowledging that fact should not reveal classified information. Conversely, if responsive records existed independently of an NSA-operated program, for example, records discussing allegations concerning a program elsewhere in government; the FOIA process could potentially identify those records and subject them to normal classification and referral procedures.</p>
<p>The appeal specifically requested that NSA reconsider the Glomar response, “explicitly confirm or deny the existence of records related to ‘Immaculate Constellation,’” process any non-exempt portions, and provide appropriate justification for information that remained withheld.</p>
<p>NSA has now rejected that argument.</p>

<a href='https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_14-09-30.jpg'><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="594" height="767" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_14-09-30.jpg" class="attachment-full size-full" alt="" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_14-09-30.jpg 594w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_14-09-30-232x300.jpg 232w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_14-09-30-150x194.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_14-09-30-450x581.jpg 450w" sizes="(max-width: 594px) 100vw, 594px" /></a>
<a href='https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_14-09-48.jpg'><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="581" height="759" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_14-09-48.jpg" class="attachment-full size-full" alt="" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_14-09-48.jpg 581w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_14-09-48-230x300.jpg 230w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_14-09-48-150x196.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-09-17_14-09-48-450x588.jpg 450w" sizes="(max-width: 581px) 100vw, 581px" /></a>
<p><strong>NSA: Even Acknowledging Records Could Harm National Security</strong></p>
<p>In its appeal determination, NSA said its original response was appropriate and again declined to answer whether responsive records exist.</p>
<p>The appeal authority wrote:</p>
<p>“I determined that NSA&#8217;s response to not confirm or deny the existence of records was appropriate.”</p>
<p>The agency then explained the reasoning behind maintaining the Glomar response:</p>
<p>“To respond otherwise would require NSA to confirm or deny the existence of an alleged intelligence program (‘Immaculate Constellation’).”</p>
<p>NSA continued:</p>
<p>“If the NSA were to confirm or deny the existence of records about an alleged NSA program to you, NSA would have to do the same for others.”</p>
<p>According to the agency, releasing that information could “expose or compromise classified and protected intelligence information, sources, methods, or activities,” potentially harming national security and undermining NSA&#8217;s mission.</p>
<p>The appeal determination also directly addressed the argument concerning another agency&#8217;s public statements.</p>
<p>NSA wrote:</p>
<p>“Another agencies&#8217; acknowledgement of the existence or non-existence of records regarding an alleged program does not require NSA to acknowledge the existence or non-existence of records about the same program.”</p>
<p>The appeal was therefore denied.</p>
<p><strong>What the NSA Response Does — and Does Not — Establish</strong></p>
<p>NSA&#8217;s decision creates no evidentiary support for the underlying Immaculate Constellation allegations.</p>
<p>A Glomar response cannot logically be read as “yes.” By design, it provides neither a confirmation nor a denial.</p>
<p>That limitation is particularly important because NSA explicitly stated in its original response that this is its standard treatment for requests seeking intelligence records or intelligence-related activity, even that which is involving UFOs and UAP.</p>
<p>Consequently, the NSA correspondence provides no basis for concluding that the agency operated Immaculate Constellation, participated in it, knows that it exists, or possesses any of the extraordinary imagery or intelligence described in the whistleblower-origin report.</p>
<p>The documentary record remains considerably narrower.</p>
<p>A report alleging the program&#8217;s existence was provided to Congress and formally entered into a House hearing record. The report contains detailed allegations concerning imagery intelligence, HUMINT reporting, signals intelligence and government UAP activities. DoD has publicly stated that it has “no record, present or historical” of a SAP called Immaculate Constellation. And NSA refuses to say whether it possesses intelligence records pertaining to the allegation.</p>
<p>Those facts can coexist without confirming the alleged program.</p>
<p><strong>The Continuing Problem With Glomar</strong></p>

<p>The NSA case also illustrates a recurring transparency problem created by Glomar responses.</p>
<p>There is an important distinction between asking whether a classified intelligence program exists and asking whether an intelligence agency possesses records concerning allegations about such a program. The latter could encompass material originating outside NSA, correspondence discussing press reports, intelligence reporting, interagency communications, referrals or other records without establishing that the alleged program itself exists.</p>
<p>Indeed, the ODNI record demonstrates that this distinction is not merely hypothetical. ODNI possessed an internal document discussing the Immaculate Constellation allegations, summarizing press reporting and recording DoD&#8217;s denial. Its existence did not establish that the alleged program was real.</p>
<p>NSA nevertheless maintains that even acknowledging the existence or nonexistence of intelligence records responsive to the request would reveal protected information.</p>
<p>That leaves a fundamental question unresolved: if an allegation is false, and an agency truly possesses no responsive intelligence records, what national-security interest would be damaged by simply saying so?</p>
<p>NSA&#8217;s appeal determination does not provide a case-specific public explanation answering that question. Instead, it states that confirming or denying records concerning alleged intelligence programs could expose classified intelligence information, sources, methods or activities, and that another agency&#8217;s acknowledgement does not bind NSA.</p>
<p>That is the government&#8217;s legal position. It is not evidence that Immaculate Constellation exists.</p>
<p>For now, the publicly available evidence remains insufficient to substantiate the extraordinary allegations contained in the whistleblower report. NSA&#8217;s Glomar response does nothing to change that evidentiary picture. It establishes only that NSA will not say whether responsive intelligence records exist, even after another component of the U.S. government publicly stated that it has no record of the alleged program.</p>
<p>The distinction between a government record documenting an allegation and government documentation proving the allegation remains essential in evaluating Immaculate Constellation. At present, the public record establishes the former, but not the latter.</p>

										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/benghazi.jpg"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="alignright size-medium wp-image-21455" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/benghazi-300x200.jpg" alt="" width="300" height="200" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/benghazi-300x200.jpg 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/benghazi-150x100.jpg 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/benghazi-450x300.jpg 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/benghazi-768x512.jpg 768w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/benghazi-600x400.jpg 600w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/benghazi.jpg 1024w" sizes="(max-width: 300px) 100vw, 300px" /></a>On the night of September 11, 2012, armed attackers assaulted U.S. government facilities in Benghazi, Libya, beginning at the State Department’s Special Mission compound and continuing into the early hours of September 12 at a nearby CIA annex. Four Americans were killed: U.S. Ambassador to Libya J. Christopher Stevens, State Department Information Management Officer Sean Smith, and security personnel Tyrone Woods and Glen Doherty. The attack occurred amid a deteriorating security environment in post-revolution Libya, where the central government had limited control over numerous armed militias and extremist organizations. The State Department’s independent Accountability Review Board later reported that there had been no specific tactical warning of the September 11 attack, while also identifying serious security deficiencies and broader shortcomings in the government’s understanding of the threat environment in eastern Libya.</p>
<p>In the months and years that followed, the circumstances surrounding Benghazi became the subject of extensive investigations by the State Department, FBI, intelligence community and multiple congressional committees. Those inquiries examined separate but overlapping questions, including security conditions before the attack, requests and arrangements for protecting U.S. personnel, intelligence concerning extremist activity, the U.S. government’s response while the attacks were underway, and the public explanation of what had occurred. The State Department’s Accountability Review Board ultimately issued 29 recommendations, 24 of them unclassified, addressing diplomatic security and the protection of personnel at high-risk posts. The Department stated that it accepted all of the Board’s recommendations.</p>
<p>The Black Vault’s Benghazi archive brings together records obtained through the Freedom of Information Act, government releases, congressional investigations and other document collections so researchers can examine the underlying record directly. A substantial portion of the archive concerns one of the central questions raised after the attack: what information the U.S. government possessed about threats in Benghazi and the surrounding region before September 11, 2012. The collection includes FOIA responses and records from the Defense Intelligence Agency, Department of Defense, FBI, CIA and National Security Agency. Some requests produced released records, while others identified responsive material that remained classified or otherwise withheld. The NSA collection, for example, contains internal government communications concerning the developing situation during the attack, while other agency responses document records that were located but withheld from public disclosure.</p>
<p>The archive also documents how information about Benghazi was communicated inside the government and presented publicly after the attack. Included are the State Department’s September 2012 “Rapid Response: Hot Topics” newsletters, records concerning FBI interviews of survivors, White House emails, and several collections tracing the development and revision of the government’s Benghazi “talking points.” Among these are records released by the White House as well as approximately 100 pages obtained by The Black Vault from the Office of the Director of National Intelligence containing communications involving the intelligence community, State Department, CIA and other officials. Taken together, these records allow researchers to compare successive drafts and communications rather than relying solely on later descriptions of how the government’s public account developed.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/libya-us-embassy-attack.avif"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="alignleft size-medium wp-image-21456" src="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/libya-us-embassy-attack-300x168.avif" alt="" width="300" height="168" srcset="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/libya-us-embassy-attack-300x168.avif 300w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/libya-us-embassy-attack-150x84.avif 150w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/libya-us-embassy-attack-450x252.avif 450w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/libya-us-embassy-attack-768x431.avif 768w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/libya-us-embassy-attack-600x337.avif 600w, https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/libya-us-embassy-attack.avif 770w" sizes="(max-width: 300px) 100vw, 300px" /></a>Additional material addresses security at the Benghazi facilities and the physical aftermath of the attack. The archive includes officially released photographs from the attack scene and a 130-page State Department collection concerning Blue Mountain Group, the security contractor that provided local guards at the Special Mission compound. Other FBI and intelligence-agency FOIA responses illustrate the limits of the public record: in several instances agencies acknowledged locating responsive records concerning threats or warnings but withheld some or all of the underlying material under FOIA exemptions involving classified national-security information, statutory protections or law-enforcement proceedings. Those responses are preserved alongside the released records so that the scope of what remains unavailable is also part of the archive.</p>
<p>Finally, the collection includes material from the congressional investigations that followed Benghazi, including the report of the House Select Committee on the Events Surrounding the 2012 Terrorist Attack in Benghazi, released in June 2016. That investigation examined events preceding the attack, the military and civilian response on September 11 and 12, and government communications afterward. Because Benghazi generated years of political dispute and competing claims about responsibility, intelligence, security and the government’s public statements, the purpose of this archive is not to resolve those disputes for the reader. The document index below instead preserves the available primary records, FOIA responses, investigative material and related releases so they can be reviewed directly and compared against the claims that have surrounded the attack.</p>

<h3>Report of the Select Committee on the Events Surrounding the 2012 Terrorist Attack in Benghazi</h3>
<p><em>Published 6/28/2016</em></p>
<p><em><strong>Washington, D.C.</strong> – Select Committee on Benghazi <strong>Chairman Trey Gowdy (SC-04)</strong> released the following statement after the committee’s Majority released a mark of its investigative report:</em></p>
<p><em>“Chris Stevens, Sean Smith, Glen Doherty and Tyrone Woods were heroes who gave their lives in service to our country. Their bravery and the courageous actions of so many others on the ground that night should be honored.</em></p>
<p><em>“When the Select Committee was formed, I promised to conduct this investigation in a manner worthy of the American people’s respect, and worthy of the memory of those who died. That is exactly what my colleagues and I have done.</em></p>
<p><em>“Now, I simply ask the American people to read this report for themselves, look at the evidence we have collected, and reach their own conclusions. You can read this report in less time than our fellow citizens were taking fire and fighting for their lives on the rooftops and in the streets of Benghazi.”</em></p>
<p><em>The committee’s proposed report is just over 800 pages long and is comprised of five primary sections and 12 appendices. It details relevant events in 2011 and 2012.</em></p>
<p><em><u>The following facts are among the many new revelations in Part I:</u></em></p>
<ul>
<li><em>Despite President Obama and Secretary of Defense Leon Panetta’s clear orders to deploy military assets, nothing was sent to Benghazi, and nothing was en route to Libya at the time the last two Americans were killed almost 8 hours after the attacks began. [pg. 141]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>With Ambassador Stevens missing, the White House convened a roughly two-hour meeting at 7:30 PM, which resulted in action items focused on a YouTube video, and others containing the phrases “[i]f any deployment is made,” and “Libya must agree to any deployment,” and “[w]ill not deploy until order comes to go to either Tripoli or Benghazi.” [pg. 115]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>The Vice Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff typically would have participated in the White House meeting, but did not attend because he went home to host a dinner party for foreign dignitaries. [pg. 107]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>A Fleet Antiterrorism Security Team (FAST) sat on a plane in Rota, Spain, for three hours, and changed in and out of their uniforms four times. [pg. 154]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>None of the relevant military forces met their required deployment timelines. [pg. 150]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>The Libyan forces that evacuated Americans from the CIA Annex to the Benghazi airport was not affiliated with any of the militias the CIA or State Department had developed a relationship with during the prior 18 months. Instead, it was comprised of former Qadhafi loyalists who the U.S. had helped remove from power during the Libyan revolution. [pg. 144]</em></li>
</ul>
<p><em><strong>Rep. Mike Pompeo (KS-04)</strong> released the following statement regarding these findings:</em></p>
<p><em>“We expect our government to make every effort to save the lives of Americans who serve in harm’s way. That did not happen in Benghazi. Politics were put ahead of the lives of Americans, and while the administration had made excuses and blamed the challenges posed by time and distance, the truth is that they did not try.”</em></p>
<p><em><strong>Rep. Martha Roby (AL-02)</strong> released the following statement regarding these findings:</em></p>
<p><em>“Our committee’s insistence on additional information about the military’s response to the Benghazi attacks was met with strong opposition from the Defense Department, and now we know why. Instead of attempting to hide deficiencies in our posture and performance, it’s my hope our report will help ensure we fix what went wrong so that a tragedy like this never happens again.” </em></p>
<p><em><u>The following facts are among the many new revelations in Part II:</u></em></p>
<ul>
<li><em>Five of the 10 action items from the 7:30 PM White House meeting referenced the video, but no direct link or solid evidence existed connecting the attacks in Benghazi and the video at the time the meeting took place. The State Department senior officials at the meeting had access to eyewitness accounts to the attack in real time. The Diplomatic Security Command Center was in direct contact with the Diplomatic Security Agents on the ground in Benghazi and sent out multiple updates about the situation, including a “Terrorism Event Notification.” The State Department Watch Center had also notified Jake Sullivan and Cheryl Mills that it had set up a direct telephone line to Tripoli. There was no mention of the video from the agents on the ground. Greg Hicks—one of the last people to talk to Chris Stevens before he died—said there was virtually no discussion about the video in Libya leading up to the attacks. [pg. 28]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>The morning after the attacks, the National Security Council’s Deputy Spokesperson sent an email to nearly two dozen people from the White House, Defense Department, State Department, and intelligence community, stating: “Both the President and Secretary Clinton released statements this morning. … Please refer to those for any comments for the time being. To ensure we are all in sync on messaging for the rest of the day, Ben Rhodes will host a conference call for USG communicators on this chain at 9:15 ET today.” [pg. 39]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>Minutes before the President delivered his speech in the Rose Garden, Jake Sullivan wrote in an email to Ben Rhodes and others: “There was not really much violence in Egypt. And we are not saying that the violence in Libya erupted ‘over inflammatory videos.’” [pg. 44]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>According to Susan Rice, both Ben Rhodes and David Plouffe prepared her for her appearances on the Sunday morning talk shows following the attacks. Nobody from the FBI, Department of Defense, or CIA participated in her prep call. While Rhodes testified Plouffe would “normally” appear on the Sunday show prep calls, Rice testified she did not recall Plouffe being on prior calls and did not understand why he was on the call in this instance. [pg.98]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>On the Sunday shows, Susan Rice stated the FBI had “already begun looking at all sorts of evidence” and “FBI has a lead in this investigation.” But on Monday, the Deputy Director, Office of Maghreb Affairs sent an email stating: “McDonough apparently told the SVTS [Secure Video Teleconference] group today that everyone was required to ‘shut their pieholes’ about the Benghazi attack in light of the FBI investigation, due to start tomorrow.” [pg. 135]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>After Susan Rice’s Sunday show appearances, Jake Sullivan assured the Secretary of the State that Rice “wasn’t asked about whether we had any intel. But she did make clear our view that this started spontaneously and then evolved.” [pg. 128]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>Susan Rice’s comments on the Sunday talk shows were met with shock and disbelief by State Department employees in Washington. The Senior Libya Desk Officer, Bureau of Near Eastern Affairs, State Department, wrote: “I think Rice was off the reservation on this one.” The Deputy Director, Office of Press and Public Diplomacy, Bureau of Near Eastern Affairs, State Department, responded: “Off the reservation on five networks!” The Senior Advisor for Strategic Communications, Bureau of Near East Affairs, State Department, wrote: “WH [White House] very worried about the politics. This was all their doing.” [pg. 132]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>The CIA’s September 13, 2012, intelligence assessment was rife with errors. On the first page, there is a single mention of “the early stages of the protest” buried in one of the bullet points. The article cited to support the mention of a protest in this instance was actually from September 4. In other words, the analysts used an article from a full week before the attacks to support the premise that a protest had occurred just prior to the attack on September 11. [pg. 47]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>A headline on the following page of the CIA’s September 13 intelligence assessment stated “Extremists Capitalized on Benghazi Protests,” but nothing in the actual text box supports that title. As it turns out, the title of the text box was supposed to be “Extremists Capitalized on Cairo Protests.” That small but vital difference—from Cairo to Benghazi—had major implications in how people in the administration were able to message the attacks. [pg. 52]</em></li>
</ul>
<p><em><strong>Rep. Jim Jordan (OH-04)</strong> released the following statement regarding these findings:</em></p>
<p><em>“Obama Administration officials, including the Secretary of State, learned almost in real time that the attack in Benghazi was a terrorist attack. Rather than tell the American people the truth, the administration told one story privately and a different story publicly.”</em></p>
<p><em><strong>Rep. Peter Roskam (IL-06)</strong> released the following statement regarding these findings:</em></p>
<p><em>“In the days and weeks after the attacks, the White House worked to pin all of the blame for their misleading and incorrect statements on officials within the intelligence community, but in reality, political operatives like Ben Rhodes and David Plouffe were spinning the false narrative and prepping Susan Rice for her interviews.”</em></p>
<p><em><u>The following facts are among the many new revelations in Part III:</u></em></p>
<ul>
<li><em>During deliberations within the State Department about whether and how to intervene in Libya in March 2011, Jake Sullivan listed the first goal as “avoid[ing]a failed state, particularly one in which al-Qaeda and other extremists might take safe haven.” [pg. 9]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>The administration’s policy of no boots on the ground shaped the type of military assistance provided to State Department personnel in Libya. The Executive Secretariats for both the Defense Department and State Department exchanged communications outlining the diplomatic capacity in which the Defense Department SST security team members would serve, which included wearing civilian clothes so as not to offend the Libyans. [pg. 60]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>When the State Department’s presence in Benghazi was extended in December 2012, senior officials from the Bureau of Diplomatic Security were excluded from the discussion. [pg. 74]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>In February 2012, the lead Diplomatic Security Agent at Embassy Tripoli informed his counterpart in Benghazi that more DS agents would not be provided by decision makers, because “substantive reporting” was not Benghazi’s purpose. [pg. 77]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>Emails indicate senior State Department officials, including Cheryl Mills, Jake Sullivan, and Huma Abedin were preparing for a trip by the Secretary of State to Libya in October 2012. According to testimony, Chris Stevens wanted to have a “deliverable” for the Secretary for her trip to Libya, and that “deliverable” would be making the Mission in Benghazi a permanent Consulate. [pg. 96]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>In August 2012—roughly a month before the Benghazi attacks—security on the ground worsened significantly. Ambassador Stevens initially planned to travel to Benghazi in early August, but cancelled the trip “primarily for Ramadan/security reasons.” [pg. 99]</em></li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li><em>Former Secretary of Defense Leon Panetta bluntly told the committee “an intelligence failure” occurred with respect to Benghazi. Former CIA Deputy Director Michael Morell also acknowledged multiple times an intelligence failure did in fact occur prior to the Benghazi attacks. [pg. 129]</em></li>
</ul>
<p><em><strong>Rep. Susan Brooks (IN-05)</strong> released the following statement regarding these findings:</em></p>
<p><em>“President Obama has said his worst mistake was ‘failing to plan for the day after … intervening in Libya.’ As a result of this ‘lead from behind’ foreign policy, the Libyan people were forced to make the dismal trade of the tyranny of Qadhafi for the terror of ISIS, Al-Qaeda and others. Although the State Department considered Libya a grave risk to American diplomats in 2011 and 2012, our people remained in a largely unprotected, unofficial facility that one diplomatic security agent the committee interviewed characterized as ‘a suicide mission.’”</em></p>
<p><em><strong>Rep. Lynn Westmoreland (GA-03)</strong> released the following statement regarding these findings:</em></p>
<p><em>“One of the most concerning parts of the State Department’s policy in Libya was its reliance upon the militias of an unstable nation to protect our men and women in Benghazi. These were by no means forces that could adequately protect Americans on the ground, and the State Department knew it. But the appearance of no boots on the ground was more important to the administration.”</em></p>
<p><em>Part IV of the report reveals new information about the Select Committee’s requests and subpoenas seeking documents and witnesses regarding Benghazi and Libya, and details what the Obama administration provided to Congress, what it is still withholding, and how its serial delays hindered the committee’s efforts to uncover the truth.</em></p>
<p><em>Part V proposes 25 recommendations for the Pentagon, State Department, Intelligence Community, and Congress aimed at strengthening security for American personnel serving abroad and doing everything possible to ensure something like Benghazi never happens again, and if it does, that we are better prepared to respond.</em></p>
<p><em>The Select Committee intends to convene a bipartisan markup to discuss and vote on the proposed report on July 8, 2016. All members of the committee will have the opportunity to offer changes in a manner consistent with the rules of the House.</em></p>

<h3><strong>P<a name="progressreport"></a>rogress Report on Benghazi Terror Attack Investigation</strong></h3>
<p>Last year, Speaker John Boehner asked the House committees on Armed Services, Foreign Affairs, Intelligence, Judiciary, and Oversight &amp;
