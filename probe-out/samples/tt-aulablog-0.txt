<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva’s victory in Brazil’s 2026 presidential runoff offers an opportunity to reflect on the strategies of mobilization capable of challenging the far right in a Latin American context marked by the growing strength of conservative forces. The result does not signal the end of Bolsonarismo, nor does it halt the rightward shift observed across the region. It does, however, demonstrate that this trajectory is not inevitable. In Brazil, the electoral contest was shaped by three interconnected dimensions that help explain political mobilization: <strong>streets, networks, and fear</strong>.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Streets: Political Mobilization and Intergenerational Engagement</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The response to the first-round results began at universities. At Fluminense Federal University (UFF), in Niterói, students organized a demonstration on October 5, one day after the vote. Initially coordinated through WhatsApp groups and student organizations, the mobilization drew thousands of people and inspired demonstrations at more than 40 universities across 14 states and the Federal District. What began as a localized reaction developed into a national public mobilization (<a href="https://www.brasildefato.com.br/2026/10/08/estudantes-vao-as-ruas-puxam-onda-de-protestos-contra-flavio-bolsonaro-e-ampliam-atos-pro-lula">Brasil de Fato</a>⁠).</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The significance of these demonstrations went beyond the number of participants. The streets became spaces for organizing, circulating messages, and revitalizing political engagement. Students mobilized classmates, relatives, and local communities, connecting the presidential race to concrete experiences. These included the impacts of funding cuts at federal universities during Jair Bolsonaro’s administration and concerns about the future of public policies.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This mobilization also had an intergenerational dimension. Reports published during the campaign described young people encouraging elderly relatives to vote and accompanying those with disabilities to polling stations.
