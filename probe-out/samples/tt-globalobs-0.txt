										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p>In July 2024, Chinese and Russian bombers <a href="https://www.fdd.org/analysis/russian-and-chinese-strategic-bombers-patrol-near-alaska/">flew</a> a joint patrol just off Alaska. Two months later, Chinese coast guard vessels <a href="https://news.usni.org/2024/10/03/china-coast-guard-now-operating-in-the-bering-sea">sailed</a> with Russian ships in the Bering Sea, farther north than Chinese coast guard vessels had been observed before. In 2025, US threats to annex Greenland showed how quickly Arctic security can turn into great-power politics.<a href="#_ftn1" name="_ftnref1">[1]</a> This year, <a href="https://www.defensenews.com/news/your-military/2026/09/21/us-watching-chinese-marine-presence-off-alaska-arctic-coast-guard-chief-says/">four Chinese research vessels</a> worked off Alaska, some as far north as the North Pole, and an October Congressional Research Service <a href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R49487">report</a> detailed Russia’s Arctic military buildup. Yet Arctic security has never become a recurring subject of deliberation in the General Assembly or Security Council.</p><p>No existing forum brings together the eight Arctic states, Indigenous representatives, and other interested actors to discuss what changes in the Arctic mean for international security. This leaves a space for the UN. The Arctic is not absent from the UN system. It appears in <a href="https://www.unep.org/resources/emerging-issues/shrinking-arctic-sea-ice">environmental work</a>, <a href="https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/safety/pages/polar-code.aspx">maritime regulation</a>, and the <a href="https://www.asil.org/insights/volume/25/issue/8">technical process governing continental-shelf claims</a>. What is missing is a recurring agenda item on Arctic security in the General Assembly or Security Council. The Security Council, where Russia and the United States hold vetoes, may not be a realistic venue.</p><p>But the General Assembly could provide a low-cost political forum where Arctic states, including Russia, could discuss the security consequences of a fast-warming region, from new competition over seabed resources and sea routes to military incidents and the role of Indigenous peoples in decisions about the region.<strong><span id="more-26765"></span></strong></p><p><strong>Arctic Security Is Absent by Choice</strong></p><p>For an issue to reach the agenda of the General Assembly or Security Council, member states have to push for it to be there. A clear example is the effort of the <a href="https://www.un.org/en/node/97649">Alliance of Small Island States</a> (AOSIS) to advance climate action in UN spaces. Founded in 1990, AOSIS unites small island states around the shared concern that sea-level rise <a href="https://www.un.org/ohrlls/content/bureau-aosis">threatens their existence</a>. The Alliance has pursued that concern in the climate negotiations; in the UN development agenda, leading to the <a href="https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/198168">1994 Barbados Programme of Action</a>; and in the General Assembly, where its Pacific members secured the adoption of Resolution <a href="https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/656156/files/A_RES_63_281-EN.pdf">63/281 in 2009</a>, which asked the secretary-general to report on climate and security.</p><p>Even when member states push, they do not always succeed. In December 2021, for example, an Irish and Nigerien <a href="https://press.un.org/en/2021/sc14732.doc.htm">draft resolution</a> linking climate change and security won 12 of 15 Council votes but was vetoed by Russia, with India also voting no and China abstaining. In the case of Arctic security, however, there has been no equivalent effort to begin with. Arctic security is absent from the agenda not because it has been blocked but because Arctic states have chosen not to put it there.</p><p>Arctic states have long preferred to handle the region among themselves. Diplomats from two Arctic states spoken with for this article said that to their knowledge their governments had never been asked to sponsor an Arctic security item at the UN. In the 2008 Ilulissat Declaration, five Arctic coastal states (Canada, Denmark, Norway, Russia, and the United States) directly affirmed that there was “<a href="https://cil.nus.edu.sg/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/2008-Ilulissat-Declaration.pdf">no need to develop a new comprehensive international legal regime to govern the Arctic Ocean</a>.”</p><p><strong>Arctic States Have Other Options </strong></p><p>None of the institutions that currently handle Arctic governance bring together all the Arctic states to address security in the region. The Arctic Council, established in 1996, was deliberately given a limited <a href="https://1997-2001.state.gov/global/oes/oceans/declaration.html">mandate</a>. It has no legal personality and no power to make binding decisions, and a footnote to its founding declaration states that it “should not deal with matters related to military security.” The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) and the Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf handle seabed claims for the states that are parties to the convention, which the United States is not. <a href="https://www.nato.int/en/what-we-do/deterrence-and-defence/arctic-security">NATO handles collective defense</a> for seven of the eight Arctic states.<a href="https://www.thearcticinstitute.org/country-backgrounders/european-union/"> The </a><a href="https://www.thearcticinstitute.org/country-backgrounders/european-union/">European Union</a> sets policy for Denmark, Sweden, and Finland, and its single-market rules reach Norway and Iceland through the European Economic Area.
