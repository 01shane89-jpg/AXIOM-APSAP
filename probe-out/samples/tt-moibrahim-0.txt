  <title>Should I stay or should I go? What Africa’s young people are telling us about migration</title>

      &lt;div class&gt;
        &lt;p&gt;For a young African finishing university, looking for a first job, or trying to build a career, a question emerges: Should I stay or should I go? For young people, the possibility of migration often coincides with some of the most important &lt;a href="https://doi.org/10.5772/intechopen.1003529" target="_blank"&gt;transitions&lt;/a&gt; into adulthood – pursuing further education, entering the labour market, achieving financial independence, starting a family. … When opportunities to navigate these transitions are limited at home, seeking options abroad becomes a practical alternative.&amp;nbsp;&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p&gt;On a continent where young people continue to face high levels of &lt;a href="https://mo.ibrahim.foundation/sites/default/files/2020-05/2019-forum-report_0.pdf" target="_blank"&gt;unemployment&lt;/a&gt;, leaving can be a compelling option. According to Afrobarometer’s latest flagship report, &lt;a href="https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Beyond-borders-Afrobarometer-flagship-report-English-digital.pdf" target="_blank"&gt;Beyond borders: Citizen perspectives on a shifting global order&lt;/a&gt;, 45% of Africans have considered emigrating; among 18- to 35-year-olds, 32% have thought “a lot” about leaving.&amp;nbsp;&lt;/p&gt;&lt;h4&gt;Migration as a governance barometer&amp;nbsp;&lt;/h4&gt;&lt;p&gt;The reasons people give for wanting to emigrate point to a broader governance challenge. When asked why they are considering leaving, potential emigrants overwhelmingly point to economic concerns: Half (50%) say they would leave in search of work, while 29% cite a desire to escape poverty or economic hardship.&amp;nbsp;&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p&gt;These motivations matter because they point beyond individual choices to the economic and governance conditions in which those choices are being made. Migration, in this sense, can be read as a barometer of how citizens perceive the opportunities available to them.&amp;nbsp;&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p&gt;For many young Africans, the problem begins before they enter the workforce. The Mo Ibrahim Foundation’s &lt;a href="https://mo.ibrahim.foundation/sites/default/files/2019-03/2019-Forum-Report.pdf" target="_blank"&gt;2019 Ibrahim Forum Report &lt;/a&gt;highlights a mismatch between education and employers’ needs across the continent, noting that higher levels of education don’t necessarily translate into better job prospects. In 11 African countries surveyed by the International Labour Organization, 32.2% of unemployed young people identified a lack of jobs as their main obstacle to finding work, while 29.9% cited high job requirements or insufficient work experience. The transition from education to employment can be difficult, leaving young people with qualifications but limited opportunities to put them to use.&amp;nbsp;&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p&gt;This makes the migration aspirations of educated Africans particularly significant. Among those with post-secondary education, 31% say they have thought “a lot” about emigrating, compared to 18% of those with no formal schooling. The very education that should expand young people's opportunities can, in the absence of corresponding employment opportunities, expand their awareness of what they could pursue elsewhere.&amp;nbsp;&lt;/p&gt;&lt;h4&gt;When opportunity lies beyond Africa&amp;nbsp;&lt;/h4&gt;&lt;p&gt;The aspirations of potential emigrants are also becoming increasingly global. While more than half of actual &lt;a href="https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Beyond-borders-Afrobarometer-flagship-report-English-digital.pdf" target="_blank"&gt;African migrants&lt;/a&gt; remain on the continent, the destinations that potential emigrants say they would prefer tell a more outward-looking story. Among potential emigrants, the share who would prefer to move elsewhere in Africa has fallen from 36% in 2016/2018 to 26% in 2024/2025. Meanwhile, 32% would opt for Europe and 28% for North America, while another 16% identify destinations in regions such as the Middle East, Asia, and Oceania.&amp;nbsp;&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p&gt;&lt;a href="https://doi.org/10.5772/intechopen.1003529" target="_blank"&gt;Research&lt;/a&gt; shows that this outward focus may be shaped by growing digital connectivity. Access to mobile phones, the Internet, and social media exposes young Africans to opportunities abroad, as well as to the experiences of successful migrants and the benefits associated with remittances.&amp;nbsp;&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p&gt;This shift in preferred destinations coincides with persistent barriers to movement within Africa. Across 38 countries surveyed by Afrobarometer, 56% of Africans support free movement of people across international borders, yet 63% say that crossing borders for work or trade is difficult in practice. In other words, there is a gap between Africans' support for greater mobility and their experience of moving across borders.&amp;nbsp;&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p&gt;That gap matters for the continent's broader integration ambitions. The &lt;a href="https://au-afcfta.org/purpose-the-afcfta/" target="_blank"&gt;African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA)&lt;/a&gt; is intended to enable the free flow of goods and services across the continent and boost the trading position of Africa in the global market, yet the initiative remains largely unfamiliar to the citizens it is ultimately meant to benefit. Only 13% of Africans say they have heard of the AfCFTA, and awareness does not exceed 27% in any of the 38 surveyed countries.&amp;nbsp;&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p&gt;If young Africans are looking for opportunities across borders, the question is whether Africa's integration agenda is serving them in ways they can see, understand, and use.&amp;nbsp;&lt;/p&gt;&lt;h4&gt;Migration: Risk or opportunity?&amp;nbsp;&lt;/h4&gt;&lt;p&gt;The desire to migrate should not be reduced to a rejection of Africa or a problem that governments must solve by keeping young people at home.
